# Module 2: Estimating & Bidding APIs — Exam

> Exam for **Module 2: Estimating & Bidding APIs** (The Industry) — 25 questions, pass mark 80%. Correct answers are marked ✅ (read from each question's own post record; no quiz attempt was started to get them).

### Q1. Your AI builds an estimating API that stores material prices as static values in the code. Lumber prices shift 20% in a month. What is the consequence?

- **A.** Every estimate generated after the price shift is inaccurate because the API uses outdated cost data for materials  ✅
- **B.** The API will crash when it encounters a price mismatch between the hardcoded value and the supplier invoice total
- **C.** Material suppliers will reject purchase orders generated by the platform because the prices do not match current rates
- **D.** The estimate appears correct locally but fails validation when submitted to the regional pricing authority for review

> **Answer:** A

### Q2. A GC receives quotes from four electrical subcontractors. Each quote covers slightly different scope. How should the platform help compare them?

- **A.** Sort the four quotes by total price from lowest to highest and recommend the cheapest option available
- **B.** Average all four quote amounts and use the mean price as the electrical line item in the overall project estimate
- **C.** Reject any quote that is more than ten percent above the lowest bid since the outlier is likely overpriced for scope
- **D.** Normalize quotes to the same scope and exclusions through bid leveling so the GC can compare on an equal basis  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q3. Your estimating API uses JavaScript floating-point math for all financial calculations. An estimate with 500 line items totals one cent off. Why?

- **A.** The browser rounds display values differently than the server and the total appears incorrect only in the interface
- **B.** One of the 500 line items has a unit cost entered with more than two decimal places causing a rounding mismatch
- **C.** Floating-point math produces tiny rounding errors that compound across hundreds of calculations in the estimate  ✅
- **D.** The database stores financial values with limited decimal precision and truncates fractions during the save process

> **Answer:** C

### Q4. A change order adds $45,000 of additional electrical work. The platform processes the change but does not update the contract value. What breaks?

- **A.** The subcontractor payment schedule becomes misaligned because the original payment milestones are not adjusted
- **B.** Final billing will not reconcile because the total billed amount exceeds the original contract value on record  ✅
- **C.** The project schedule extends without manager approval because the change order scope triggers additional new tasks
- **D.** The owner gets a duplicate invoice for the change order amount because the system creates parallel billing

> **Answer:** B

### Q5. Your progress billing API calculates the amount due but does not withhold retainage. The owner rejects the pay application. Why?

- **A.** Retainage is standard in construction billing and owners expect a percentage withheld until the project is substantially complete  ✅
- **B.** The pay application format is missing required fields that the owner accounting system needs to process the payment
- **C.** The billing amount exceeds the approved monthly maximum that the owner set in the original financing agreement
- **D.** The owner approval workflow requires a physical signature on paper and does not accept electronic pay applications

> **Answer:** A

### Q6. Your API generates an estimate but does not log who created it or when edits were made. Six months later a bid dispute arises. What is the problem?

- **A.** The estimate cannot be reopened because the system archives all estimates after thirty days without an audit trail
- **B.** The bid dispute is automatically decided in favor of the other party because your estimate lacks formal certification
- **C.** The estimating system will need to be rebuilt from scratch to add audit logging before the dispute can be resolved
- **D.** There is no record of who entered the original numbers, when changes were made, or who approved the final bid  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q7. Your cost database shows concrete at $145 per cubic yard in Dallas. A project in Seattle uses the same price. What did the platform get wrong?

- **A.** The platform should convert the price from US dollars to the local currency used in the Seattle construction market
- **B.** Material costs vary by region and the platform should apply Seattle pricing instead of using the Dallas default rate  ✅
- **C.** The $145 rate is a wholesale price and the platform should add a standard retail markup before using it in an estimate
- **D.** Concrete pricing is negotiated per project and no database rate should be used as a starting point for any estimate

> **Answer:** B

### Q8. An estimate line item reads: 120 CY of 4000 PSI concrete at $145/CY with an extension of $17,500. Is the calculation correct?

- **A.** Yes, the extension is correct because 120 multiplied by $145 rounds to $17,500 when displayed at the summary level
- **B.** The extension is close enough for estimating purposes since construction estimates routinely allow rounding tolerance
- **C.** No, 120 times $145 equals $17,400 and the $100 discrepancy indicates a calculation error the API must not produce  ✅
- **D.** The extension includes a standard 5% waste factor that the platform automatically adds to all concrete line items

> **Answer:** C

### Q9. Your AI builds a change order form that lets the PM submit additional work without requiring owner approval in the workflow. What is the risk?

- **A.** Unapproved changes get billed to the owner who disputes charges because they never authorized the added work  ✅
- **B.** The change order triggers an automatic extension to the schedule that the PM cannot reverse after submission
- **C.** The subcontractor begins work based on the unapproved change order and claims a lien if payment is later denied
- **D.** The insurance policy becomes void because unauthorized scope changes invalidate coverage on the project

> **Answer:** A

### Q10. Your billing API generates pay applications in a custom PDF format. The owner bank requires AIA G702/G703 format. What happens?

- **A.** The bank accepts the custom format but processes the payment more slowly due to the additional manual review required
- **B.** The custom format is automatically converted to AIA standard by the banking system during the processing step
- **C.** The owner can override the bank requirement and approve payment using any format they choose on the project
- **D.** The pay application is rejected because AIA G702/G703 is the construction billing standard that banks require  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q11. Your estimate API calculates line item extensions correctly but the division subtotals do not match the sum of their line items. What is wrong?

- **A.** The division subtotals include a hidden overhead percentage that the API adds automatically at the division level
- **B.** The line items were edited after the subtotals were cached and the system has not recalculated the rollup values
- **C.** The rollup calculation at the division level is not aggregating all child line items correctly in the hierarchy logic  ✅
- **D.** Some line items belong to multiple divisions simultaneously and are being double-counted in the subtotal calculation

> **Answer:** C

### Q12. A subcontractor submits their quote by email rather than through the platform. How should the platform handle this off-system bid for leveling?

- **A.** Reject the email quote entirely and require the subcontractor to resubmit through the platform before any comparison
- **B.** Allow the estimator to manually enter the email quote into the leveling system so it can be compared alongside other bids  ✅
- **C.** Forward the email to an AI parser that automatically extracts the bid amount and inserts it into the comparison table
- **D.** Accept the email quote as the winning bid since the subcontractor demonstrated initiative by submitting it directly

> **Answer:** B

### Q13. Your API pricing lookup fails because the cost database server is unreachable. The estimate is due in two hours. What should the system do?

- **A.** Substitute zero for all prices where the lookup failed so the estimator sees exactly which line items are missing
- **B.** Block the entire estimate from being submitted until every single pricing lookup has succeeded and returned a value
- **C.** Generate the estimate using the last known prices and mark the document as final without any pricing disclaimers
- **D.** Return the last cached prices with a clear warning flag so the estimator can proceed but knows values may be stale  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q14. Your progress billing shows the project is 60% complete. The retainage rate is 10%. Total contract value is $500,000. What is the correct amount currently due?

- **A.** Earned to date is $300,000 minus 10% retainage of $30,000 leaving $270,000 billable less any previous payments  ✅
- **B.** The full $300,000 is billable now and the retainage is deducted only from the final payment at project completion
- **C.** Earned to date is $300,000 minus the full $50,000 retainage calculated on the total contract value not just earned
- **D.** The billing amount depends entirely on the owner approval and the platform should not calculate it automatically

> **Answer:** A

### Q15. Your estimating API supports line items but not a hierarchical structure with divisions and subdivisions. Why does this matter for construction?

- **A.** Flat line item lists are easier to manage and the hierarchical structure adds unnecessary complexity to the system
- **B.** Construction estimates organize work by trade division and managers need rollup subtotals at each hierarchy level  ✅
- **C.** Regulatory bodies require estimates to be formatted in CSI division structure before they can be submitted for permits
- **D.** Subcontractors will not participate in a platform that does not match the organizational format they use internally

> **Answer:** B

### Q16. Your change order workflow skips the cost estimation step and goes directly from request to owner approval. What is the risk?

- **A.** The owner approves the change but the subcontractor refuses to perform the work without a formal cost agreement
- **B.** The approval is invalid without a cost estimate because construction contracts require a dollar amount on every change
- **C.** The owner approves work without knowing the cost impact which creates a billing dispute when the invoice arrives  ✅
- **D.** The project schedule cannot accommodate the change because the system needs a cost estimate to calculate duration

> **Answer:** C

### Q17. A subcontractor bid includes exclusions that the other three bids cover. The platform shows all four bids side by side by total price only. What is the problem?

- **A.** The excluded scope items will cause a permit delay because the building inspector will flag the missing work during review
- **B.** The platform should automatically add a cost penalty to bids with exclusions to normalize them against complete bids
- **C.** The GC cannot submit the bid package to the owner until all subcontractor quotes cover exactly the same line items
- **D.** Comparing only on total price hides scope gaps and the GC may select a cheaper bid that actually costs more overall  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q18. Your estimate includes a 15% markup on all line items for overhead and profit. The API applies the markup to each line item individually. Is this correct?

- **A.** It depends on the contract, some apply markup per line item while others apply it as a single percentage on the subtotal  ✅
- **B.** Markup should always be applied per line item because that is the only method recognized by construction accounting
- **C.** Markup should always be applied at the estimate total level because per-line markup inflates the apparent item costs
- **D.** Markup should not be visible in the estimate at all and should only appear on the final invoice to the project owner

> **Answer:** A

### Q19. Your billing API tracks current billing but not previous payments or cumulative billed amounts. Why does this make pay applications incorrect?

- **A.** The owner accounting team cannot process a pay application that does not include the project start date and number
- **B.** Banks require fully cumulative billing data to track loan disbursement progress against the total construction financing
- **C.** Without previous payment tracking the API cannot calculate the net amount due and may bill for work already paid for  ✅
- **D.** The retainage calculation requires cumulative data because the withheld percentage changes at different milestones

> **Answer:** C

### Q20. Your AI builds the estimating API to accept quantity input as free-text strings like "about 120 yards" instead of structured numeric fields. What is the problem?

- **A.** The cost database cannot look up unit prices when the unit of measure is embedded in a free-text description string
- **B.** Free-text quantities cannot be multiplied by unit costs reliably because the system must parse text into numbers first  ✅
- **C.** Estimators prefer free-text entry because it is faster and the system should accommodate their workflow preferences
- **D.** The audit trail is weaker with free-text input because the system cannot track exactly when numeric values changed

> **Answer:** B

### Q21. Your platform allows estimates to be edited after bid submission without creating a new version or logging the changes. What risk does this create?

- **A.** The original bid price cannot be verified if a dispute arises because the post-submission edits overwrote the record  ✅
- **B.** The platform performance degrades over time because unversioned edits create database fragmentation in the system
- **C.** The owner sees the edited estimate and may question publicly why the numbers changed after the official bid was submitted
- **D.** The subcontractor quotes linked to the estimate become invalid because they reference the pre-edit line item values

> **Answer:** A

### Q22. Your retainage logic releases the full withheld amount at 100% completion. The standard practice is substantial completion. What is the difference?

- **A.** Substantial completion means the owner has signed a formal acceptance letter certifying every item on the punch list
- **B.** There is no practical difference because substantial completion and 100% completion occur at the same project point
- **C.** Substantial completion triggers only a partial retainage release and the remainder is held until one year after close
- **D.** Substantial completion means the building is usable for its intended purpose even if minor punch list items remain  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q23. Your estimate API calculates material cost correctly but does not account for waste factor. A concrete pour estimate is 10% short on site. What was missing?

- **A.** The delivery truck capacity does not match the order quantity and the remaining material was left at the batch plant
- **B.** A waste factor multiplier that accounts for spillage, formwork loss, and over-excavation in the quantity calculation  ✅
- **C.** The supplier delivered a lower-strength concrete mix that requires a larger volume to meet the structural requirement
- **D.** The blueprint dimensions were measured incorrectly and the actual pour area is larger than the estimated footprint

> **Answer:** B

### Q24. A GC submits a bid using your platform. The owner asks for a detailed cost breakdown by trade division. Can the platform provide this?

- **A.** Only if the GC manually creates a separate report because the estimating system stores data as a flat line item list
- **B.** No, detailed breakdowns by division are confidential and should never be shared with the owner during the bid process
- **C.** Yes, if the estimate uses a hierarchical structure with divisions then the system can generate rollup reports by trade  ✅
- **D.** The owner should hire their own independent estimator to verify the bid rather than requesting a contractor breakdown

> **Answer:** C

### Q25. You audit your construction estimating platform and every calculation checks out. How often should financial accuracy be re-verified going forward?

- **A.** After every update to calculation logic and periodically because pricing database changes can affect estimate accuracy  ✅
- **B.** Only after a customer reports a billing discrepancy since a passing audit proves the system is calculating correctly
- **C.** Once per fiscal year during the annual accounting review when all financial systems are reconciled for tax reporting
- **D.** Financial verification is no longer needed unless the platform migrates to an entirely new database engine or hosting platform
