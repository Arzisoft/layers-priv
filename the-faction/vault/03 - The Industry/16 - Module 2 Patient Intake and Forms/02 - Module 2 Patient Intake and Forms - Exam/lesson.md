# Module 2: Patient Intake and Forms — Exam

> Exam for **Module 2: Patient Intake and Forms** (The Industry) — 25 questions, pass mark 80%. Correct answers are marked ✅ (read from each question's own post record; no quiz attempt was started to get them).

### Q1. A family medicine practice launches digital intake that opens with a long medication history screen, and 35 percent of patients quit before finishing. The office manager asks what to change first. What do you direct?

- **A.** Require patients to create a portal account first so partial answers are saved to their record before questions begin
- **B.** Move the medication history to a printed sheet at check-in and keep the digital flow for demographics and consents only
- **C.** Break the medication history into shorter steps after the quick demographics, and save progress as each step completes  ✅
- **D.** Shorten the medication list to only the five most common drug classes, since a briefer form is always completed more often

> **Answer:** C

### Q2. An orthopedic group plans to hand every patient a tablet at the front desk to complete all intake on arrival. The administrator asks whether that solves the clipboard problem. What do you tell them?

- **A.** It works if the practice buys enough devices that patients never have to wait for a tablet during the busy morning check-in rush
- **B.** Send the intake link at booking or in the reminder so patients finish at home, and keep check-in for review and same-day items  ✅
- **C.** Keep paper packets for older patients and tablets for younger ones so each group completes intake in its preferred format
- **D.** Move intake to a kiosk in the waiting room so staff stop handing out devices and patients complete forms while they wait

> **Answer:** B

### Q3. A pediatric practice makes parents redo the entire intake packet at every well-child visit, and parents are frustrated. How do you redesign what gets re-asked?

- **A.** Re-ask only the layers that go stale, like medications and allergies, and have parents confirm demographics, not retype them  ✅
- **B.** Re-ask the full packet once a year at the first visit of the year, so every record layer gets refreshed on the same schedule
- **C.** Stop re-asking anything after the first visit, since the chart already holds history and staff can update it during the visit
- **D.** Let parents choose which sections to update each visit so each family controls how much time intake takes before the visit

> **Answer:** A

### Q4. A multi-provider family practice keeps seeing claims denied because the name or birth date on the claim does not match the payer record. The billing lead blames the clearinghouse. Where do you say the fix belongs?

- **A.** In the clearinghouse settings, since a well-configured scrubber should catch mismatched identifiers before claims go out
- **B.** In the billing workflow, since a pre-submission review of every claim would catch identifier mismatches before submission
- **C.** In intake, where card capture and exact-as-printed member details create the clean identifiers claims depend on later  ✅
- **D.** In the payer portals, since staff can correct the payer's record whenever the practice holds the accurate version of it

> **Answer:** C

### Q5. A dermatology practice revises its financial policy and asks whether consents patients signed under the old policy still apply. What does your consent design need in order to handle this?

- **A.** Version every consent document, store the exact text each patient signed with a timestamp, and prompt re-signature on changes  ✅
- **B.** Store one master copy of each consent and update it in place so the practice always presents the current policy language to patients
- **C.** Collect fresh signatures on every consent at every visit so no signature on file is ever more than one appointment old
- **D.** Keep signed consents as scanned images in each chart so staff can always produce proof that the patient signed something

> **Answer:** A

### Q6. At a pediatric practice, a grandmother brings her grandchild to an appointment and begins signing the consent forms on the check-in tablet. What must your intake design already handle?

- **A.** Blocking anyone except a parent from completing any intake step until the practice reaches a parent by phone for direct approval
- **B.** Accepting the signature as valid, since the adult physically present with the child is responsible for that day's visit
- **C.** Skipping consent collection for minors entirely and having the provider document verbal consent inside the visit note
- **D.** Capturing who is signing and their relationship to the child, and routing legal consents to an authorized parent or guardian  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q7. A family practice's intake link is opened mostly on older phones, and abandonment spikes midway through the flow. Which design direction do you give when specifying the rebuild?

- **A.** Detect the device and redirect older phones to a simplified PDF version patients can download, print at home, and bring to check-in
- **B.** One question per screen, large touch targets, a visible progress indicator, and save-and-resume so interruptions cost nothing  ✅
- **C.** A desktop-first layout that scales down, since a design built for the largest screens adapts cleanly to the smallest ones
- **D.** A native app for iOS and Android, since installed apps handle weak connections better than browser-based intake flows can

> **Answer:** B

### Q8. Returning patients at an internal medicine clinic complain that they retype their full medical history at every visit. What do you direct AI to build?

- **A.** A branch at the start of the flow so returning patients confirm and update stored answers while new patients get full history  ✅
- **B.** A shorter universal form for everyone, trimming history questions so no patient, new or returning, faces the full packet again
- **C.** An autofill feature that pulls each patient's history from the payer's records so nobody enters clinical data manually
- **D.** A staff-assisted step where the front desk pre-fills history from the chart and patients only sign to confirm at check-in

> **Answer:** A

### Q9. You are designing intake for a group practice with OB/GYN and orthopedic providers. A partner suggests one shared questionnaire to keep the build simple. How do you respond?

- **A.** Agree, since a single shared questionnaire is easier to maintain and staff only learn one intake flow across the group
- **B.** Build two completely separate intake systems so each specialty fully controls its own flow with no shared infrastructure
- **C.** Use the shared form but add an open comments box where patients can describe specialty concerns the fixed questions missed
- **D.** Share the core demographic and insurance screens, then branch by specialty so each patient sees only relevant questions  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q10. A completed intake form currently lands in the front desk email inbox as a PDF, and staff retype every answer into the practice management system. What do you fix in the build?

- **A.** The PDF layout, formatting it to mirror the PMS screens so staff retype answers faster with fewer transcription errors
- **B.** The inbox workflow, adding rules that sort intake PDFs into folders by appointment date so nothing gets lost before entry
- **C.** The staffing plan, assigning a dedicated team member to data entry so retyping happens consistently and never piles up
- **D.** The import path, delivering intake as structured data through CSV, API, or summary formats the PMS can actually accept  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q11. A solo dermatologist wants digital intake live in two weeks, has no technical staff, and wants someone else handling hosting and uptime. What do you recommend?

- **A.** Configure a managed form platform, trading some control for speed and hosting the practice never has to think about  ✅
- **B.** Direct AI to build a fully custom intake app, since custom builds are the only reliable way to hit a two-week launch
- **C.** Wait until the practice hires technical staff, since any intake system fails without someone inside maintaining it daily
- **D.** Build custom but host it on the office computer so the dermatologist keeps full local ownership of all patient data

> **Answer:** A

### Q12. An office manager hands you a wish list of 15 extra intake questions about hobbies, referral sources, and household details, in case they are useful someday. What do you advise?

- **A.** Cut every field nobody downstream actually reads, because each added question costs completion rate across the form  ✅
- **B.** Add them as a final optional section so the practice gets data from motivated patients without requiring it from anyone
- **C.** Add them all now, since collecting more data up front is cheaper than redesigning the flow later when new needs appear
- **D.** Rotate the extra questions so each patient sees only five, spreading the collection burden evenly across the panel

> **Answer:** A

### Q13. Patients completing a pediatric practice's intake at home get interrupted constantly, and many links are opened twice but never finished. What capability do you prioritize?

- **A.** A countdown timer showing how long the form should take, motivating patients to finish in one sitting before stepping away
- **B.** Save-and-resume, so a patient who steps away returns to exactly where they left off instead of facing a blank form again  ✅
- **C.** A short session window that expires quickly, prompting patients to restart while the appointment is still top of mind
- **D.** Automatic text reminders every few hours after an unfinished session, nudging the patient until the form finally gets done

> **Answer:** B

### Q14. A billing team complains that intake keeps handing them payer names like BCBS and blue cross tx that they cannot verify. What do you change in the insurance screens?

- **A.** Add a validation rule requiring payer names spelled out in full, rejecting abbreviations before the form can ever be submitted
- **B.** Add a note asking patients to copy the payer name exactly from their card so billing receives the official spelling
- **C.** Route every submission to the front desk for manual correction, since staff know the local payers better than patients
- **D.** Replace the free-text field with a payer picker backed by card capture, so payer identity arrives structured and verifiable  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q15. An urgent care clinic's front desk types insurance card details by hand into intake records, and member ID typos keep surfacing weeks later as denials. What do you direct AI to add?

- **A.** A double-entry step where patients type the member ID twice, catching typos whenever the two entries fail to match
- **B.** A larger font on the member ID field with formatting hints, making the number easier to read back and verify while typing
- **C.** A daily audit report listing all IDs entered that day so a second staff member reviews each one against the card copies
- **D.** Camera-based card capture with OCR that reads the card into structured fields, taking hand transcription out of the loop  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q16. A family practice moves nearly all intake pre-visit and asks you what should still happen at the front desk when the patient arrives. What belongs at check-in?

- **A.** Nothing at all, since a well-designed pre-visit flow removes any reason for the front desk to touch intake data again
- **B.** Verification tasks, confirming identity, re-checking the insurance card, collecting copays, and catching same-day changes  ✅
- **C.** The full medical history review, since clinical questions deserve the privacy of the office rather than a patient's home
- **D.** Consent signatures, since signatures collected in person on office hardware carry more weight than remote electronic ones

> **Answer:** B

### Q17. You are specifying which intake layers a returning-patient flow should always re-confirm. A colleague argues everything ages at the same rate. Which layer goes stale fastest and needs review at every visit?

- **A.** Demographics, since address and phone changes are the most common updates practices miss between patient visits
- **B.** Medications and allergies, which change constantly and carry clinical risk when the chart quietly falls out of date  ✅
- **C.** Insurance details, which typically change with each calendar year and drive most of the avoidable claim denials
- **D.** Consent signatures, which expire on a fixed schedule and must be recollected before every scheduled appointment

> **Answer:** B

### Q18. A practice's first digital intake attempt was a single scrolling page with 40 required questions, and patients abandoned it until staff brought the clipboards back. What went wrong?

- **A.** The questions were required, and making every field optional would have let patients finish and staff fill gaps by phone
- **B.** The form went digital too soon, and the practice needed a year of tablet intake at the desk before moving fully online
- **C.** The launch lacked patient training, and a walkthrough video sent with the link would have carried patients through it
- **D.** The build recreated the paper clipboard on a screen instead of a sequenced, branching flow designed for completion  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q19. A patient disputes what they agreed to in a telehealth consent signed eight months ago, and the practice can only produce the current consent text. What did the intake build get wrong?

- **A.** It failed to require re-signature at every visit, which would have kept the newest signature only weeks old at any time
- **B.** It stored signatures without witness confirmation, which is what gives an electronic consent its evidentiary weight
- **C.** It kept the signature but not the signed artifact, the stored version of the exact text the patient agreed to that day  ✅
- **D.** It relied on electronic signatures at all, when disputed consents are exactly why paper originals belong in the chart

> **Answer:** C

### Q20. A dermatology clinic photographs lesions during visits, and providers sometimes discover mid-appointment that no photo consent exists. Where do you place photo consent in your design?

- **A.** In the pre-visit intake flow, versioned and timestamped, so consent is signed before the provider enters the room  ✅
- **B.** In the provider's workflow, since the person taking the photo is best positioned to explain and capture consent live
- **C.** In an annual consent packet mailed each January, keeping all specialty consents current for the full calendar year
- **D.** In the after-visit summary, where patients review what was photographed and confirm consent once they get home

> **Answer:** A

### Q21. Testing a new OB/GYN intake flow, you watch a patient struggle to type an insurance ID and a due date on a five-year-old Android phone. What is the design lesson?

- **A.** Set a minimum supported device standard and show unsupported phones a message directing patients to call the desk
- **B.** Older devices need a stripped-down text-only version of the flow served separately from the full modern experience
- **C.** Inputs must match the data, date pickers, numeric keypads, and camera capture, and the flow must pass on low-end phones  ✅
- **D.** Complex fields like insurance IDs belong at the front desk where staff with full keyboards enter them accurately

> **Answer:** C

### Q22. An intake flow captures insurance beautifully, but the practice still discovers inactive coverage when claims come back denied weeks after visits. What is missing?

- **A.** A patient attestation checkbox confirming coverage is active, shifting responsibility for accuracy onto the subscriber
- **B.** Eligibility verification run before the visit, so coverage problems surface while there is still time to resolve them  ✅
- **C.** A secondary insurance field, since most surprise denials trace to coordination of benefits rather than inactive plans
- **D.** Monthly re-capture of card images, keeping payer data fresh enough that coverage lapses cannot slip through unnoticed

> **Answer:** B

### Q23. A multi-provider group's PMS offers a documented API, but the office manager suggests staff download a weekly CSV instead because that is familiar. How do you frame the choice?

- **A.** Choose the CSV, since familiar staff workflows beat technical elegance and a weekly batch keeps intake data fresh enough
- **B.** Choose both from day one, running the API and the weekly CSV in parallel so each import can be checked against the other
- **C.** Favor the API where it exists, since same-day structured delivery into the PMS beats a manual weekly batch staff must run  ✅
- **D.** Choose neither and keep intake in its own database, giving the practice one clean system of record it fully controls

> **Answer:** C

### Q24. A practice needs deep conditional branching, custom specialty questionnaires, and tight control over every screen, and the managed platforms you tried keep hitting limits. What is the right call?

- **A.** A custom AI-directed build, accepting responsibility for hosting and upkeep in exchange for control platforms cannot give  ✅
- **B.** Stacking two managed platforms, using one for the branching sections and another for the questionnaires it does best
- **C.** Simplifying requirements until a managed platform fits, since branching depth is rarely worth leaving managed hosting
- **D.** Licensing the PMS vendor's built-in intake module, since native tools always integrate more deeply than external ones

> **Answer:** A

### Q25. You are finalizing your Module 2 approach before proposing intake builds to real practices. What principle should govern every intake design decision you make?

- **A.** Capture the broadest possible dataset at intake, since data collected once is an asset the practice can mine for years
- **B.** Match the intake flow to the practice's existing paper forms so staff and patients face the least change at launch
- **C.** Design for completion and clean downstream data, collecting only what the practice uses, when it is best collected  ✅
- **D.** Standardize one intake flow across all specialties and patient types so the system stays simple to maintain over time

> **Answer:** C
