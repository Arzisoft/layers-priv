# Module 1: The Clinical Practice Workflow Map — Exam

> Exam for **Module 1: The Clinical Practice Workflow Map** (The Industry) — 25 questions, pass mark 80%. Correct answers are marked ✅ (read from each question's own post record; no quiz attempt was started to get them).

### Q1. A new family medicine client asks you to map their patient journey before any build begins. Which sequence correctly orders the pipeline from first contact through follow-up?

- **A.** First contact, scheduling, pre-visit intake, check-in, clinical encounter, checkout, documentation, billing, follow-up  ✅
- **B.** Scheduling, first contact, check-in, pre-visit intake, clinical encounter, documentation, checkout, billing, and follow-up
- **C.** First contact, pre-visit intake, scheduling, check-in, clinical encounter, billing, documentation, checkout, follow-up
- **D.** First contact, scheduling, check-in, pre-visit intake, clinical encounter, billing, checkout, documentation, follow-up

> **Answer:** A

### Q2. You are designing a checkout-stage tool for a dermatology practice. To keep staff from rejecting it within a week, which workflow reality matters most as you specify the build?

- **A.** Which payer contracts the practice renegotiated this year, since reimbursement rates ultimately determine any tool's value
- **B.** How each provider personally prefers to document visits, since clinical style shapes every stage of the patient pipeline
- **C.** Which EHR vendor the practice might switch to eventually, since the future platform decides how the tool must be designed
- **D.** What happens directly upstream and downstream of checkout, since a tool that ignores those handoffs gets rejected quickly  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q3. During discovery at a three-provider OB/GYN practice, you are sorting tasks into lanes on your swimlane map. Which task belongs to the back office rather than the front office?

- **A.** Answering inbound phone calls and booking appointment slots, the daily scheduling work that keeps provider calendars full
- **B.** Following up on denied claims with payers after visits are billed, the work that recovers revenue once patients have left  ✅
- **C.** Collecting copays from patients at the front check-in desk, the moment the practice first takes payment for the day's visit
- **D.** Checking patients in and confirming their demographic details, the desk work that starts every appointment on schedule

> **Answer:** B

### Q4. An orthopedic group asks you to walk their new manager through how the practice actually gets paid. Which chain states the revenue cycle in the correct order?

- **A.** Visit, eligibility check, claim submission, charge capture, payer adjudication, patient balance collection, and payment posting
- **B.** Eligibility check, visit, charge capture, claim submission, payer adjudication, payment posting, patient balance collection  ✅
- **C.** Eligibility check, charge capture, visit, claim submission, payment posting, payer adjudication, patient balance collection
- **D.** Charge capture, eligibility check, visit, payer adjudication, claim submission, payment posting, patient balance collection

> **Answer:** B

### Q5. A dermatology office manager wants you to automate the handling of one-off billing disputes that require careful judgment on every case. How do you respond?

- **A.** Agree and build it first, since honoring the client's stated priority is how a builder earns their trust
- **B.** Automate it partially, letting AI settle the simple disputes alone and routing only the hardest cases to billing staff
- **C.** Build it after a compliance review, since dispute handling touches protected data and needs formal approval before work
- **D.** Redirect toward repetitive high-volume work instead, since rare judgment-heavy edge cases make poor automation targets  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q6. An urgent care clinic hands you four candidate automations and asks which to build first. Applying the leverage test, which target should top your list?

- **A.** A tool drafting responses to rare, complex billing disputes, since those cases frustrate the manager more than any other
- **B.** Automated appointment reminders and recall outreach, high-volume repetitive work where every miss costs the clinic money  ✅
- **C.** A tool suggesting clinical treatment options to providers, since the encounter is the highest-value moment of the visit
- **D.** A tool for negotiating payer contract terms each year, since reimbursement rates set the ceiling on practice revenue

> **Answer:** B

### Q7. You are ranking five bottlenecks gathered from discovery interviews at a group practice. Which three factors should drive your leverage ranking?

- **A.** Staff enthusiasm for the tool, provider seniority, and how visible the win will be to the practice's ownership group
- **B.** Ease of building the tool, how modern the practice's software is, and how soon its vendor contract is up for renewal
- **C.** Volume of the task, how repetitive the task is, and the cost of a single error when the task is done wrong or missed  ✅
- **D.** Loudness of the complaint, the number of staff who mention it, and how long the problem has existed at the practice

> **Answer:** C

### Q8. You are opening discovery at a busy pediatric practice. Knowing how pressure points shift by specialty, which operational areas should you probe first?

- **A.** Vaccine schedule tracking and parent communication, the recurring workload that dominates pediatric operations  ✅
- **B.** Imaging orders and surgical scheduling coordination, the workload that typically overwhelms procedural specialties
- **C.** Cosmetic service upsells and photo documentation flows, the operational rhythm that drives most visit revenue here
- **D.** Walk-in triage surges and rapid room turnover, the throughput pattern that defines episodic acute care operations

> **Answer:** A

### Q9. An orthopedic group brings you in to map its workflow. Which pressure points, distinctive to this specialty, should you expect to dominate the map?

- **A.** Vaccine schedules and parent messaging volume, since recurring well-visit cadences shape this specialty
- **B.** Recurring prenatal visit sequences with hard deadlines, since date-driven care plans dominate procedural specialties
- **C.** Same-day walk-in demand and unpredictable volume swings, since episodic acute traffic defines specialty group flow
- **D.** Imaging, inbound referrals, and surgical scheduling, the coordination-heavy work that drives orthopedic operations  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q10. An OB/GYN practice asks why your discovery checklist differs from the one you used at an urgent care clinic. What defining OB/GYN pattern explains the difference?

- **A.** Unpredictable walk-in surges, where staffing the front desk for peak demand is the practice's dominant daily challenge
- **B.** Recurring visit sequences with hard deadlines, where a missed appointment in the series carries real clinical stakes  ✅
- **C.** Referral-driven patient acquisition, where inbound specialist coordination controls the flow of new appointments
- **D.** Vaccine schedule administration, where dose timing rules generate most of the practice's daily outreach workload

> **Answer:** B

### Q11. You built a recall outreach tool for a family medicine office, and now an urgent care clinic wants the same thing. Why should you hesitate before reusing it as is?

- **A.** Urgent care clinics are barred from patient outreach under payer rules, so a recall tool creates immediate legal exposure
- **B.** Urgent care software cannot connect to outside tools, so the build would have to replace the clinic's entire system
- **C.** Family medicine tools are tuned for smaller patient volumes, so the clinic's daily traffic would overwhelm the design
- **D.** Urgent care runs on episodic walk-in visits rather than ongoing patient relationships, so its pressure points differ  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q12. Reviewing your swimlane map for a family medicine practice, you need to flag the compliance-sensitive steps. Which set of moments should carry the flags?

- **A.** Anywhere patient information is collected, stored, transmitted, or used to communicate about a patient's ongoing care  ✅
- **B.** Only the steps inside the clinical encounter itself, since regulation stops at the exam room door in outpatient care
- **C.** Only the billing and claims steps, since regulators focus on money movement rather than the handling of patient data
- **D.** The scheduling steps alone, since appointment data is the most sensitive information a medical practice ever handles

> **Answer:** A

### Q13. While mapping intake for a pediatric group, you realize the tool you plan to propose would store patient insurance details. As the builder, what is your correct move at this stage?

- **A.** Drop the feature entirely, since builders should never propose tools that come anywhere near protected information
- **B.** Proceed without noting it, since compliance is the practice's responsibility and sits outside a builder's project scope
- **C.** Flag the touchpoint on the map and design around it now, then dig into the detailed compliance requirements later  ✅
- **D.** Pause the whole engagement until the practice hires a compliance officer to formally approve your workflow map

> **Answer:** C

### Q14. An office manager gives you a confident verbal tour of the practice's workflow. Before you finalize your map, what should you do next?

- **A.** Watch the work actually happen at the desks, since a manager's description and the staff's reality often disagree  ✅
- **B.** Send the map straight to the providers for sign-off, since clinicians hold the most accurate view of daily operations
- **C.** Compare the tour against industry benchmarks for the specialty and finalize whichever version looks more standard
- **D.** Ask the manager to repeat the tour in writing, since documented answers are more reliable than spoken interviews

> **Answer:** A

### Q15. After a rambling ninety-minute interview with a practice administrator, you have pages of scattered notes. Following the Module 1 toolkit, what do you do with them?

- **A.** File the notes and schedule more interviews until every staff answer converges, since early maps are always premature
- **B.** Draft the swimlane map entirely by hand first, since manual drafting is how a builder proves domain understanding
- **C.** Direct AI to synthesize the notes into a workflow map and ranked bottleneck list you review with the manager  ✅
- **D.** Send the raw notes back to the administrator to correct line by line, since accuracy must precede any synthesis

> **Answer:** C

### Q16. At a family medicine practice, the front desk loudly blames the phones for everything, but your map shows a large pile of unworked denied claims. Where do you aim the first build?

- **A.** At the phones, since staff buy-in comes first and the front desk will champion a tool that removes their daily pain
- **B.** At both at once, since splitting the first build across the two problems protects you if either bet proves wrong
- **C.** At the unworked claims, since the map shows the real revenue leak even though the phones draw the loudest complaints  ✅
- **D.** At neither yet, since conflicting signals mean discovery failed and the engagement should restart from the beginning

> **Answer:** C

### Q17. You propose a digital intake tool for a dermatology practice. The office manager asks what happens to the data after patients submit it. Why is this the critical question?

- **A.** If patients see confirmation screens too late they abandon forms, and abandonment is the top failure mode of intake
- **B.** If nobody imports the data into the practice management system, the tool just moves the typing to a different desk  ✅
- **C.** If the data sits in the tool too long it goes stale, and stale demographics are the leading cause of claim denials
- **D.** If providers dislike the form's layout adoption stalls, and preference decides whether intake tools survive

> **Answer:** B

### Q18. A builder tells an administrator the new scheduling helper will "connect to the EHR." The administrator winces. What distinction did the builder most likely get wrong?

- **A.** The EHR term is outdated, and modern practices expect builders to say electronic medical record in every proposal
- **B.** Scheduling lives in the practice management system, not the EHR, and confusing the two erodes trust instantly  ✅
- **C.** The EHR only stores billing records, while scheduling and the clinical charts both live inside the practice system
- **D.** Administrators expect builders to name the exact vendor, and generic platform references signal shallow research

> **Answer:** B

### Q19. You are preparing the discovery deliverable you will review with a group practice manager. Per the Module 1 toolkit, what should this artifact look like?

- **A.** A swimlane diagram with patient, front office, provider, and back office as lanes, built by directing AI to draft it  ✅
- **B.** A feature list for the proposed tool with screenshots, pricing tiers, and a build timeline covering the first quarter
- **C.** A written compliance assessment covering every regulation that could apply to the practice's patient data handling
- **D.** A benchmarking report comparing the practice's key metrics against national averages for its size and specialty

> **Answer:** A

### Q20. A tool you tuned for a high-volume family medicine office flops at an orthopedic clinic. Which mapping mistake most likely explains the failure?

- **A.** Skipping the pricing conversation, since specialty clinics expect enterprise contracts before any pilot can begin
- **B.** Underestimating the training burden, since orthopedic staff need far longer onboarding than family medicine teams
- **C.** Choosing the wrong software vendor, since specialty clinics standardize on platforms family medicine rarely uses
- **D.** Treating every practice as identical, missing the referral-heavy rhythm that drives the specialty clinic's workflow  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q21. A multi-provider family medicine group notices patients routinely disappear after visits and never book recommended return appointments. Which pipeline stage is leaking, and why does it matter?

- **A.** The check-in stage, where slow desk work early in the visit discourages patients from ever returning to the practice
- **B.** The documentation stage, where incomplete notes keep the desk from knowing which patients still need appointments
- **C.** The follow-up and recall stage, where missed outreach quietly costs the practice visits, revenue, and continuity  ✅
- **D.** The adjudication stage, where payer delays hold up the approvals patients need before booking their next visits

> **Answer:** C

### Q22. Stepping back from Module 1, a new builder asks you for the single principle that should govern every healthcare practice engagement. What do you tell them?

- **A.** Map the workflow before you build, and aim where volume, repetitiveness, and cost of error create real leverage  ✅
- **B.** Master the leading EHR platforms before mapping, since technical fluency is what earns a builder credibility on site
- **C.** Automate whatever the staff complain about most, since visible quick wins are what keep an engagement funded
- **D.** Lead with the newest AI capabilities available, since practices hire builders to bring them the cutting edge first

> **Answer:** A

### Q23. A builder proposes one combined dashboard for "the office staff" at a multi-provider group practice. The administrator looks skeptical. What has the builder most likely missed?

- **A.** The dashboard needs sign-off from every provider before launch, because clinicians control most operational purchases
- **B.** Group practices generally buy tools that replace their practice management system rather than adding screens around it
- **C.** Front office and back office run on different staff, different software, and different pain, so one lumped tool serves nobody  ✅
- **D.** Dashboards underperform in group practices because staff there rely on spoken updates during daily morning huddles instead

> **Answer:** C

### Q24. A family medicine practice insists its billing is fine because claims always go out on time. Where in the revenue cycle should you still look for leaks first?

- **A.** The waiting room flow at check-in, since bottlenecks in patient throughput are the main driver of lost practice revenue
- **B.** The provider documentation style, since revenue problems almost always trace back to how the clinicians write notes
- **C.** The phone system capacity, since missed inbound calls are where most practices lose the bulk of collectible revenue
- **D.** The handoffs after submission, such as denials sitting unworked and payments never posted or pursued with patients  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q25. A pediatric practice discovers many visits are billed to inactive insurance plans and denied weeks later. Which revenue cycle step, moved earlier and automated, most directly prevents this?

- **A.** Eligibility verification before the visit, so coverage problems surface while there is still time to resolve them  ✅
- **B.** Payment posting after adjudication, so the billing team can spot denied claims faster and rework them the same week
- **C.** Charge capture during the encounter, so every service is coded completely before the claim ever leaves the building
- **D.** Patient balance collection at checkout, so the practice still recovers cash when payers refuse submitted claims

> **Answer:** A
