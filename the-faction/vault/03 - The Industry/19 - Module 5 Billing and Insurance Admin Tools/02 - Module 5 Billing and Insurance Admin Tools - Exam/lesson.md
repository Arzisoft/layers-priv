# Module 5: Billing and Insurance Admin Tools — Exam

> Exam for **Module 5: Billing and Insurance Admin Tools** (The Industry) — 25 questions, pass mark 80%. Correct answers are marked ✅ (read from each question's own post record; no quiz attempt was started to get them).

### Q1. A new hire at a four-provider family medicine practice asks you to sketch how a visit becomes payment so your status tracker makes sense to her. What sequence do you draw?

- **A.** Charge capture after the visit, claim creation and clearinghouse submission, payer adjudication, then posting and patient billing  ✅
- **B.** Clearinghouse review first, then charge capture, direct payer submission, patient billing, and adjudication once payment has posted
- **C.** Payer adjudication first to confirm coverage, then charge capture, clearinghouse submission, and posting once the patient has paid
- **D.** Claim creation before the visit from scheduled services, adjudication at check-in, then charge capture and posting after the payment

> **Answer:** A

### Q2. You are mapping the claim lifecycle for an orthopedics group before building a tracking dashboard. The practice manager asks what charge capture actually covers. What do you tell her?

- **A.** Collecting the patient copay at the front desk before the visit so no billable encounter leaves the office unpaid that day
- **B.** Verifying the patient's coverage and deductible with the payer so the charges are guaranteed to be reimbursed after submission
- **C.** Posting the payer's payment against the claim so the remaining patient balance can be transferred onto the next statement
- **D.** Recording the billable services and charges from a visit so the claim that follows reflects everything the practice did  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q3. A dermatology practice asks why your claim status dashboard shows a stop between their system and the payer. They thought claims went straight to insurance. What do you explain?

- **A.** The claim is being held in the payer's adjudication step, which always pauses claims for review before a payment decision
- **B.** The practice's billing system batches claims weekly, so the stop reflects claims waiting for the next scheduled upload run
- **C.** Claims route through a clearinghouse that checks and forwards them, so a claim can stall there before the payer sees it  ✅
- **D.** The stop represents the biller's internal review queue, where every claim waits for manual approval before it is submitted

> **Answer:** C

### Q4. Your dashboard for an OB/GYN group shows a claim marked as in adjudication. The office manager asks what is happening to that claim right now. What is the accurate answer?

- **A.** The clearinghouse is scrubbing the claim for missing or malformed data before it forwards the file along to the payer for review
- **B.** The payer is processing the claim against the patient's plan rules to decide whether to pay, deny, or partially pay it  ✅
- **C.** The billing team is reviewing the claim internally to confirm the codes and charges before it leaves the practice
- **D.** The payer has refused the claim, and the biller must now decide whether to correct, appeal, or write off the amount

> **Answer:** B

### Q5. A pediatric practice's biller says she spends hours on posting after remittances arrive and asks whether your build could help. Before scoping anything, what does posting mean in the lifecycle?

- **A.** Generating the month's patient statements and mailing them in one batch once all the insurance activity has fully settled
- **B.** Recording payer payments and adjustments against each claim so balances update and any patient share becomes visible  ✅
- **C.** Submitting corrected claims back through the clearinghouse after rejections so the payer can process them once more
- **D.** Reconciling the practice's bank deposits against the appointment schedule to confirm every visit produced revenue

> **Answer:** B

### Q6. You are directing AI to build a superbill generator for a family medicine clinic. The office manager asks what the finished document has to carry. What belongs on it?

- **A.** The patient's full clinical chart, visit notes, and medication history so the payer can evaluate medical decision making
- **B.** Only the total charge and a payment link, since payers and patients both just need a clean amount they can act on quickly
- **C.** The payer's adjudication results, allowed amounts, and remittance detail so the patient can see what insurance decided
- **D.** Provider and practice identifiers, patient details, the diagnosis and procedure codes staff supplied, and the charges  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q7. While scoping the superbill tool for an urgent care clinic, the owner suggests it could save time by filling in likely CPT and ICD-10 codes from the visit note. How do you respond?

- **A.** Accept it, since an AI suggestion with a human confirmation step keeps the final responsibility with the clinic's own staff
- **B.** Accept it for routine visit types only, and route anything complex or unusual to the biller for manual code selection
- **C.** Decline that piece. Codes come from clinical staff and billers, and your tool only assembles and formats what they supply  ✅
- **D.** Decline for ICD-10 but allow CPT suggestions, since procedure codes are administrative rather than clinical territory

> **Answer:** C

### Q8. A dermatology practice with many self-pay patients asks you to add one-click superbill delivery after checkout. Why do self-pay patients want the document?

- **A.** It serves as their receipt for tax filing, since the itemized codes qualify the visit for medical expense deductions
- **B.** They can submit it to their own insurer to seek reimbursement, since it carries the codes and charges a claim needs  ✅
- **C.** It locks the practice's prices in writing so the patient can dispute any later balance the office tries to collect
- **D.** It replaces the clinical visit summary, giving patients a record of the diagnoses and treatment plan for their files

> **Answer:** B

### Q9. A front desk hire at a multi-provider orthopedics group asks you the difference between a rejected claim and a denied claim while you demo the dashboard. What do you tell her?

- **A.** A rejection is the payer's final refusal, while a denial is a temporary hold that clears when coverage renews
- **B.** They are two labels for the same event, and payers simply differ in which word they print on the remittance paperwork
- **C.** A denial happens at the clearinghouse before submission, while a rejection is the payer's decision after full processing
- **D.** A rejection bounces before processing, usually for data errors, while a denial was processed by the payer and refused  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q10. Your dashboard for a pediatrics group flags a batch of claims the clearinghouse rejected for an invalid subscriber ID format. What does that mean for the workflow?

- **A.** The payer has refused payment, so the biller must draft appeals with supporting documentation before the deadlines pass
- **B.** The balances shift to the patients immediately, so statements should go out while the biller reviews the coding choices
- **C.** The claims were never processed, so staff can correct the data errors and resubmit without building an appeal case  ✅
- **D.** The claims are lost for this billing cycle, so the charges should be written off and captured on next month's submission

> **Answer:** C

### Q11. An OB/GYN practice's denial queue shows a claim denied for lack of medical necessity after full payer processing. In your tool's workflow, what should happen next?

- **A.** Auto-generate and file an appeal letter from a template, since necessity denials nearly always overturn on first appeal
- **B.** Route it to the biller's work queue with details surfaced, since deciding to rework, appeal, or write off is her call  ✅
- **C.** Correct the data fields and resubmit it automatically, since denials clear the same way clearinghouse rejections do
- **D.** Move the full balance to the patient statement immediately, since the payer has issued its final answer on the claim

> **Answer:** B

### Q12. A family medicine group wants eligibility verification built into your admin toolkit and asks when the checks should run. What timing do you specify?

- **A.** At check-in while the patient is at the desk, so the answer reflects the most current possible coverage information
- **B.** After the visit during claim creation, so the check runs against the exact services that were actually performed
- **C.** Batched days before each visit, keyed to the appointment schedule, so failures are fixed before the patient arrives  ✅
- **D.** Once a year at each patient's first visit, so staff are not repeating checks for coverage that rarely ever changes

> **Answer:** C

### Q13. The batched eligibility checks for a pediatrics group surface a mix of results: most patients verify cleanly, some fail, and some show coverage changes. What should your build do with the output?

- **A.** Present every result in a daily report so staff review all verifications equally and nothing slips past the front desk
- **B.** Message the affected patients directly that their insurance failed so they can resolve it with the payer before arriving
- **C.** Cancel the appointments with failed checks automatically so the schedule only holds visits that will be reimbursed
- **D.** Flag the failures and changes into a staff worklist tied to upcoming visits, and leave the clean verifications quiet  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q14. An orthopedics practice manager questions whether the eligibility workflow you proposed is worth the staff time it takes. What is the strongest case for it?

- **A.** Consistent verification before visits prevents the largest class of preventable denials, protecting revenue upstream  ✅
- **B.** It removes the need for a denial work queue, since verified patients cannot generate denials later in the claim lifecycle
- **C.** It lets the front desk skip collecting copays at check-in, since the payer amounts are already confirmed in advance
- **D.** It shortens payer adjudication, since claims from verified patients get processed ahead of unverified submissions

> **Answer:** A

### Q15. A multi-provider group's biller has 180 open denials and one hour a day to work them. You are designing her queue. How should it be ordered?

- **A.** By dollar value and filing or appeal deadline, so the hour goes to the claims worth the most that expire the soonest  ✅
- **B.** By payer name alphabetically, so she can batch her calls to each insurance company and reuse the reference numbers
- **C.** Oldest first regardless of amount, so no claim ages out while newer, larger balances jump ahead of it in the queue
- **D.** By denial reason category, so she resolves every claim of one type before switching contexts over to the next category

> **Answer:** A

### Q16. You demo two denial dashboard designs to a dermatology group: a chart showing 200 unworked denials by month, and a prioritized daily work queue. Which do you build first, and why?

- **A.** The chart, since leadership needs the aggregate trend far more than the biller needs another list to work through
- **B.** The chart first, since the practice cannot prioritize a queue until months of denial data have been visualized
- **C.** The queue. A count of unworked denials is a guilt trip, while a sorted list of today's next actions is a usable tool  ✅
- **D.** Both at equal depth from day one, since a dashboard is not complete until every audience has its own fully dedicated view

> **Answer:** C

### Q17. A family medicine group loses money when denials sit past payer appeal deadlines. What should you direct the AI to add to the denial tracker?

- **A.** A weekly summary email of total denial counts so leadership can remind the billing team to keep the backlog moving
- **B.** Aging alerts that escalate claims as filing and appeal deadlines approach, so time-sensitive work surfaces on its own  ✅
- **C.** An automatic appeal submission step for any claim within ten days of deadline, so nothing expires while it sits unworked
- **D.** A rule that writes off any denial older than sixty days, so the queue stays short enough for one biller to manage

> **Answer:** B

### Q18. Patients at a pediatrics group routinely call confused by their statements, and many just ignore them. You are redesigning the statement. What does the new version lead with?

- **A.** The complete list of CPT and ICD-10 codes with line-item charge detail, so patients can audit every entry before paying
- **B.** A plain-language summary of what insurance paid and what the patient owes, followed by an easy way to pay the balance  ✅
- **C.** The practice's full financial policy and collections timeline, so patients understand the consequences of waiting
- **D.** The insurance company's remittance codes and adjustment reasons, so patients can direct any disputes to the payer

> **Answer:** B

### Q19. An orthopedics group's statements are clear, but patient payments still trickle in slowly by mailed check. What is the highest-impact change to the collection flow?

- **A.** Add low-friction payment options such as a digital payment link, plus card on file where the practice allows it  ✅
- **B.** Add a late fee schedule to every statement, since a visible financial penalty is what moves slow payers to act promptly
- **C.** Shift balances to a collections agency after thirty days, since third parties recover faster than practices do
- **D.** Require payment in full at check-in before visits, since balances that never exist need no chasing afterward

> **Answer:** A

### Q20. You are designing the balance reminder sequence for a family medicine practice worried about sounding like a debt collector. What shape should the sequence take?

- **A.** A short series of respectful, plain-language reminders with a payment link, escalating gently as the balance ages  ✅
- **B.** A single firm notice stating the balance and a deadline, since repetition trains patients to ignore the messages
- **C.** Daily automated texts until the balance clears, since consistent pressure converts aging balances the fastest
- **D.** Reminders that mention possible credit reporting early, since patients prioritize bills with visible consequences

> **Answer:** A

### Q21. A multi-provider group asks you to add a feature that scans visit notes and picks the billing codes, arguing it would pay for the whole build. Where does this sit relative to your scope?

- **A.** Inside it, provided the tool cites the note text behind each code so a biller could audit the choices afterward
- **B.** Inside it for established patients, since their visit history gives the AI enough context to select codes reliably
- **C.** Outside it. Code selection carries compliance consequences and belongs to certified coders and billers, not tools  ✅
- **D.** Outside it only for surgical claims, since office visit coding is simple enough for a well-directed tool to handle

> **Answer:** C

### Q22. A dermatology practice owner, tired of denials, asks for a tool that automatically writes and submits appeal letters arguing medical necessity. What do you build instead?

- **A.** A denial queue that surfaces each claim's details and deadlines, leaving the appeal argument to the biller's judgment  ✅
- **B.** The auto-appeal tool with a disclaimer that the practice reviewed each letter, shifting liability back to the owner
- **C.** An appeal generator limited to low-dollar claims, where a weak automated argument costs the practice very little
- **D.** A tool that drafts and files appeals only for denial types the practice has historically won, keeping the risk tightly contained

> **Answer:** A

### Q23. You are scoping a billing support build for an urgent care group and sorting the wish list into your lane and the biller's lane. Which request belongs in your lane?

- **A.** A rules engine that decides which denied claims are worth appealing, based on payer history and expected recovery
- **B.** A feature that selects the correct modifier for each procedure so claims stop rejecting over modifier errors
- **C.** An automated write-off routine that clears small denied balances so the aging report stays clean for leadership
- **D.** A dashboard tracking every claim's stage from submission through adjudication, turning stalls into queue items  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q24. A pediatrics group's denial dashboard shows a steady stream of denials traced to misspelled names, outdated policy numbers, and missed verifications. Where does the durable fix live?

- **A.** Upstream, in the intake and scheduling builds, since billing tools inherit the data quality those systems feed them  ✅
- **B.** In the denial queue itself, since a biller who works corrections fast enough makes the underlying error source irrelevant
- **C.** At the clearinghouse, since stronger claim scrubbing would catch the bad data before any payer could deny it
- **D.** In the statement flow, since patients who see their own data on bills will report errors the front desk missed

> **Answer:** A

### Q25. Across every billing tool in this module, superbills, denial queues, eligibility worklists, and statements, one principle separates a valuable builder from a liability. What is it?

- **A.** Automate every repetitive billing task end to end, since staff time saved is the only measure practices pay for
- **B.** Match the depth of a certified biller's knowledge, since credible tools need credentialed-level expertise inside
- **C.** Keep tools read-only against billing data, since any tool that writes to the revenue cycle creates compliance risk
- **D.** Build the visibility and the queue, and never build the billing judgment, which stays with credentialed staff  ✅

> **Answer:** D
