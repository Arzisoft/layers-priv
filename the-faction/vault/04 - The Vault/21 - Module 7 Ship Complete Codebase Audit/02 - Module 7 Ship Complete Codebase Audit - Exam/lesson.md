# Module 7: Ship: Complete Codebase Audit — Exam

> Exam for **Module 7: Ship: Complete Codebase Audit** (The Vault) — 25 questions, pass mark 80%. Correct answers are marked ✅ (read from each question's own post record; no quiz attempt was started to get them).

### Q1. An auditor is asked what separates a technical exercise from a professional audit service. According to the capstone, what makes the difference?

- **A.** The number of scanners run, since a professional service is defined mainly by the breadth of automated tooling it brings to bear
- **B.** Everything surrounding the findings: a scope that fits the budget, trust-building communication, calm delivery, and honesty about limits  ✅
- **C.** The length of the final report, since a thorough professional deliverable is distinguished chiefly by the depth of its written detail
- **D.** The severity of the findings, since an engagement only rises to a professional service once it uncovers at least one Critical issue

> **Answer:** B

### Q2. Every audit begins with scoping. What does the capstone say scoping actually protects?

- **A.** The scanner budget, since defining scope up front is what keeps the automated tooling costs within the engagement's fee ceiling
- **B.** The report length, since a tightly scoped engagement produces a shorter document that the non-technical client is likelier to read
- **C.** Both sides, since the client knows what they are getting and the auditor avoids the trap of unbounded, unprofitable, endless work  ✅
- **D.** The severity ratings, since a clear scope prevents the auditor from inflating findings that fall outside the agreed boundaries later

> **Answer:** C

### Q3. A $200 rapid audit and a comprehensive engagement scope their time very differently. How does the capstone describe the rapid audit's time allocation?

- **A.** It splits time evenly across all thirteen layers, since a fixed per-layer budget is what keeps the rapid audit consistent between clients
- **B.** It budgets minutes per layer and leans heavily on the automated pass, reserving deep manual review for the comprehensive engagement  ✅
- **C.** It spends most of its time on manual review, since the rapid audit's value lies in the attacker-mindset testing scanners cannot do
- **D.** It devotes its time to the report, since the rapid audit's deliverable is a polished document rather than the depth of the review itself

> **Answer:** B

### Q4. The capstone specifies the order of the full audit workflow. Which sequence does it prescribe?

- **A.** Manual pass, automated pass, severity, consolidation, report, running human review first so scanners only confirm what was already found
- **B.** Severity, report, automated pass, manual pass, consolidation, assigning ratings up front so the later passes slot findings into fixed tiers
- **C.** Consolidation, automated pass, manual pass, report, severity, merging first so the passes extend a single shared findings list as they go
- **D.** Automated pass, manual pass, consolidation, severity assignment, report generation, running scanners first so they steer the manual attention  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q5. The capstone says running the automated pass before the manual pass is deliberate. What is the reasoning behind that ordering?

- **A.** The automated pass is cheaper, so running it first lets the auditor stop the engagement early if the budget is exhausted before manual work
- **B.** The automated pass takes longer to run, so starting it first lets it finish in the background while a manual review proceeds in parallel
- **C.** The scan results inform where the manual attention is most needed, and the manual pass then interprets what the scan flagged for follow-up  ✅
- **D.** The automated pass is more reliable, so its findings anchor the report while the manual pass merely adds color the client finds reassuring

> **Answer:** C

### Q6. During consolidation the auditor merges results and resolves conflicts. What kind of conflict does the capstone specifically say must be resolved here?

- **A.** Conflicts between tools and observations, since two scanners or a scanner and a manual finding may report the same issue differently  ✅
- **B.** Conflicts between the client's expectations and the findings, since consolidation is where the auditor renegotiates scope with the business
- **C.** Conflicts between the fee and the effort, since consolidation is the stage where the auditor reconciles billable hours against the flat price
- **D.** Conflicts between layers, since a single finding that spans two layers must be assigned to exactly one of them before the report is written

> **Answer:** A

### Q7. The capstone says silence during an engagement breeds anxiety. What does professional communication set at the start of an audit?

- **A.** Expectations: what you will need, when they will hear from you, and a commitment to flag anything genuinely urgent immediately as it arises  ✅
- **B.** The severity thresholds, since telling the client how ratings are assigned up front stops them from challenging individual findings later
- **C.** The scanner list, since disclosing which tools will run reassures a technical client that the engagement uses industry-standard coverage
- **D.** The final price, since agreeing the fee before any work begins is what prevents the client from disputing the invoice after delivery

> **Answer:** A

### Q8. The automated pass surfaces an actively exploitable Critical on day one of a multi-day engagement. What does the capstone say the auditor should do?

- **A.** Escalate the whole audit to comprehensive, since discovering a Critical during a rapid audit proves the cheaper scope was the wrong choice
- **B.** Log it in the report and continue, since disclosing findings piecemeal breaks the structured delivery the final document is meant to provide
- **C.** Pause the engagement entirely, since an actively exploitable Critical means the audit cannot responsibly proceed until it has been fixed
- **D.** Tell that client about that one now, with initial guidance, rather than saving an actively exploitable Critical for the report days later  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q9. The capstone says the delivery conversation sets whether the client acts wisely or badly. What does it say the auditor should lead with?

- **A.** The full findings count, since opening with the total number of issues gives the client an honest measure of the work's thoroughness
- **B.** Posture and the plan, not a body count, so the client hears where they stand and what to do before they hear how many issues exist  ✅
- **C.** The most alarming Critical, since leading with the worst finding ensures the client grasps the seriousness before anything else dilutes it
- **D.** The engagement fee, since framing the value delivered against the price paid is what positions the recurring relationship from the outset

> **Answer:** B

### Q10. The capstone insists an auditor frame findings as fixable and expected. What reasoning does it give for calling vibebuilt vulnerabilities expected?

- **A.** Expected findings carry lower severity, so framing them this way lets the auditor justify the calmer ratings the report assigns to them
- **B.** Vibebuilt apps have these patterns, and that is precisely why the audit exists, so naming them as expected removes shame and enables action  ✅
- **C.** Clients pay more for expected findings, since a predictable list of issues is easier to scope a follow-on remediation engagement around
- **D.** Expected findings need no remediation, since anything the auditor anticipated in advance is by definition a known and accepted business risk

> **Answer:** B

### Q11. The capstone stresses that an audit is point-in-time. What does that specifically mean about what an audit reflects?

- **A.** It reflects the system as it was during the audit window, not a guarantee about tomorrow's code once new features and patterns ship  ✅
- **B.** It reflects only the layers the client paid to include, so a point-in-time audit is really a statement about scope rather than about timing
- **C.** It reflects a single moment of scanning, so a point-in-time audit deliberately omits the slower manual pass to capture that exact snapshot
- **D.** It reflects the auditor's opinion at delivery, so a point-in-time audit is a subjective assessment that another auditor might grade entirely differently

> **Answer:** A

### Q12. The capstone lists three things an audit is not. Which statement matches that list?

- **A.** It is not automated, not repeatable, and not comparable, since every engagement is bespoke to the single codebase it examines at the time
- **B.** It is not free, not fast, and not guaranteed, since the constraints of budget and time bound what any single engagement can uncover
- **C.** It is not technical, not detailed, and not conclusive, since the deliverable is written for a non-technical founder rather than an engineer
- **D.** It is not monitoring, not a certificate of unbreakability, and not a promise about future code, since it examines a scoped moment only  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q13. The capstone warns that overselling an audit as permanent safety carries a specific business cost. What is it?

- **A.** It triggers a second audit, since any claim of permanent safety must be independently certified by another firm before the client can rely on it
- **B.** It inflates the fee, since promising permanent safety obligates the auditor to price in an open-ended warranty the engagement cannot support
- **C.** It voids the scorecard, since a guarantee of safety contradicts the RED and YELLOW grades the same report assigns to the client's layers
- **D.** It is the fastest way to lose a client when something surfaces later, since a promise of permanent safety is broken by the next vulnerability  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q14. The capstone reframes the point-in-time nature of an audit not as a weakness but as the reason for something. What is that something?

- **A.** The higher fee, since a snapshot that expires justifies charging more per engagement to compensate for the audit's limited useful lifespan
- **B.** The narrower scope, since a point-in-time audit deliberately examines less so it can deliver its snapshot within the rapid audit's time budget
- **C.** The relationship continues, since code changes, new features ship, new patterns get generated, and the security posture drifts over time  ✅
- **D.** The automated emphasis, since capturing a precise moment favors fast scanning over the slower manual pass that would blur the snapshot

> **Answer:** C

### Q15. The capstone describes the $200 rapid audit's role in the business model. What role does it play?

- **A.** The low-friction entry point that proves value, after which the scorecard becomes the baseline and larger engagements can follow from it  ✅
- **B.** A loss leader run at cost, since the rapid audit is priced below its true expense purely to demonstrate goodwill to prospective clients
- **C.** The primary revenue source, since the volume of rapid audits sold at scale is what sustains the audit practice financially over the long run
- **D.** The comprehensive tier's replacement, since an efficient rapid audit now covers everything the older deep engagement once had to do by hand

### Q16. The capstone says the auditor positions the recurring relationship from the very first engagement. Which framing captures that positioning?

- **A.** This is a one-time snapshot, so fix what it found and there will be no need to audit again unless you rebuild the application from scratch
- **B.** This is the cheapest audit available, so lock in the rate now before the price rises, since early clients receive the most favorable terms
- **C.** This is a snapshot, here is your baseline, security is a practice not an event, and here is how we keep the score green as you grow over time  ✅
- **D.** This is the most thorough audit possible, so no further review will be necessary once every finding in this report has been fully remediated

### Q17. The capstone gives a per-layer time budget as part of scoping. Why does it tie the time budget to the engagement size specifically?

- **A.** Because a rapid audit budgets minutes per layer while a comprehensive engagement budgets deep manual review, matching effort to the fee  ✅
- **B.** Because larger engagements have more layers, so the per-layer budget scales with how many of the thirteen layers the client chose to include
- **C.** Because the scanner licenses are billed per minute, so the time budget per layer is set by the tooling cost rather than the depth of review
- **D.** Because clients expect equal time on every layer, so the budget is divided evenly and the engagement size only changes the total, not the split

### Q18. The capstone warns against starting an audit with no scope. What does it say happens to an unscoped audit?

- **A.** It produces no findings, since without a defined scope the auditor has no criteria against which to identify what counts as a vulnerability
- **B.** It violates the client contract, since engagements legally require a signed scope document before any scanning or manual review may begin at all
- **C.** It expands until it is unprofitable and still feels incomplete, since work without a boundary grows without ever reaching a natural stopping point  ✅
- **D.** It defaults to comprehensive, since an audit with no stated scope is automatically treated as the deepest and most expensive tier available

### Q19. The capstone says the goal of the delivery conversation is a particular kind of client. Which one?

- **B.** A client who can recite every finding, since a delivery succeeds only when the business has memorized the full technical detail of each issue
- **C.** A client who understands the risk here, trusts your assessment, and is ready to act, rather than one scared into paralysis or rushed bad fixes  ✅
- **D.** A client who feels the full weight of the danger, since a properly alarmed founder is the one most likely to fund that remediation work quickly

- **A.** It lowers the fee and shortens the report, since acknowledging limits reduces both the work promised and the detail the deliverable must carry
