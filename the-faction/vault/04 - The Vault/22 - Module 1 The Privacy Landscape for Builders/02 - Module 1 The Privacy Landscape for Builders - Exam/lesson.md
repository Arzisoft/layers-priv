# Module 1: The Privacy Landscape for Builders — Exam

> Exam for **Module 1: The Privacy Landscape for Builders** (The Vault) — 25 questions, pass mark 80%. Correct answers are marked ✅ (read from each question's own post record; no quiz attempt was started to get them).

### Q1. A builder says privacy is the lawyer's problem, not theirs. How does this module answer?

- **A.** Laws describe obligations, systems fulfill them: a deletion right is nothing if a system cannot delete, and that is where builders live  ✅
- **B.** Correctly: privacy is purely a legal discipline, and builders who stay out of it entirely deliver faster with no added risk to the client
- **C.** Privacy belongs to the marketing team, since notices and trust messaging are communications work rather than legal or technical work
- **D.** Privacy belongs to the hosting provider, since data protection obligations attach to whoever physically stores the bytes involved

> **Answer:** A

### Q2. A client asks a builder to confirm in writing that their product is GDPR compliant. Per this module, what should the builder do?

- **A.** Confirm it, since a builder who implemented the mechanics is the person best positioned to certify the legal result they produce
- **B.** Say what they built and what it enables, and route the legal conclusion to qualified counsel, since compliance opinions are legal opinions  ✅
- **C.** Refuse to discuss privacy at all, since any statement about data handling exposes the builder to liability better left untouched
- **D.** Charge extra for the certification, since compliance sign-off is a premium deliverable that experienced builders provide routinely

> **Answer:** B

### Q3. A US-based builder assumes GDPR is irrelevant because their client's company is American. What does this module say?

- **A.** They are right, since GDPR binds only companies with European offices and pure US businesses sit entirely outside its reach
- **B.** They are right unless the company exceeds a revenue threshold, since GDPR applies to large enterprises regardless of location
- **C.** GDPR reaches any business handling EU residents' data wherever that business sits, so user geography matters more than company address  ✅
- **D.** GDPR applies only to companies that advertise in Europe, so it is the marketing budget that determines whether the regulation ever reaches them

> **Answer:** C

### Q4. This module lists costs of getting privacy wrong beyond fines. Which set matches?

- **A.** Slower internet speeds, higher electricity bills, and increased office rent, since privacy failures degrade infrastructure broadly
- **B.** Mandatory public apologies, criminal records for all staff, and permanent industry bans in every case, however small the failure
- **C.** Loss of the company domain name, seizure of hardware, and forced open-sourcing of the codebase under standard enforcement rules
- **D.** Breaches exposing data you never need, lost deals over failed questionnaires, expensive rebuilds, and ended client relationships  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q5. A builder wants to frame privacy competence commercially. How does this module position it?

- **A.** As a competitive advantage: enterprise questionnaires, consumer trust, and rarity mean privacy-competent builders close deals others lose  ✅
- **B.** As an unavoidable tax: privacy work is pure overhead that clients resent, so competent builders hide it inside other line items
- **C.** As a loss leader: privacy work is given away free to win projects, since no client anywhere will ever pay for compliance capability directly
- **D.** As a legal shield only: the sole commercial value of privacy competence is reducing the builder's own liability on engagements

> **Answer:** A

### Q6. This module names two scoping questions to ask early on every build. Which pair?

- **A.** What is the budget, and when is the deadline, since commercial constraints determine how much privacy the project can afford
- **B.** Where are the people whose data this system handles, and what kind of data does it handle, since these determine which regimes apply  ✅
- **C.** Which framework will we use, and which hosting plan, since technical selections are what privacy obligations actually attach to
- **D.** Who are the competitors, and what do they collect, since matching industry-standard collection is what keeps a product defensibly normal

> **Answer:** B

### Q7. A builder scopes a five-user internal tool that processes health information and decides it is too small for privacy concern. What does this module say?

- **A.** Correct: privacy laws include headcount exemptions, and tools under fifty users sit outside every major privacy regime's scope entirely
- **B.** Correct, provided the tool is internal, since privacy law governs consumer products and never reaches inside an organization
- **C.** The concern is real but deferrable, since regulators announce audits in advance and small tools can add privacy when contacted
- **D.** Most privacy laws scale obligations, not existence, and a small tool handling health data can carry more risk than a big newsletter  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q8. Per this module, which question goes to a lawyer rather than being answered by the builder?

- **A.** How should the consent checkbox store its records, since data modeling for consent is a legal design decision by nature
- **B.** Which database table should hold user emails, since storage layout determines legal exposure and needs counsel's sign-off
- **C.** Whether a proposed legal basis holds and how a regulator would view a practice, since those are judgments of law, not of building  ✅
- **D.** What color the privacy notice link should be, since notice presentation is regulated down to the visual design details

> **Answer:** C

### Q9. A builder asks what GDPR and CCPA have in common despite their differences. What core ideas does this module say repeat across regimes?

- **A.** Know what data you hold, have a reason to hold it, tell people, protect it, and honor their rights over it  ✅
- **B.** Register every database with the government, encrypt with state-approved ciphers, and hire a certified officer
- **C.** Store all data domestically, ban all analytics, and obtain notarized consent on paper for every processing purpose
- **D.** Publish source code for audit, cap company size, and renew a privacy license annually with the relevant regulator

> **Answer:** A

- **B.** They are unrelated: compliance and privacy are separate fields that happen to share a course platform but share no concepts

### Q11. This module says to treat 'GDPR applies' as a build statement. What does it translate into?

- **A.** A budget line for fines, since applicability primarily means reserving money against the enforcement that will eventually arrive
- **B.** Concrete capabilities: consent capture, notice display, export, deletion, and records, which the course teaches you to build  ✅
- **C.** A marketing constraint, since applicability mainly restricts which countries the product may be advertised in going forward
- **D.** A hosting mandate, since applicability reduces to choosing a European server and everything else follows from that choice

### Q12. A builder collects extra data 'because it might be useful someday.' How does this module characterize that data?

- **A.** As an appreciating asset, since data value compounds over time and future purposes will justify today's speculative collection
- **B.** As harmless surplus, since unused data sits inert and only actively processed data can create liability for anyone involved
- **C.** As standard practice, since every serious product over-collects early and prunes later once real usage patterns emerge clearly
- **D.** As pure liability: it can leak and it cannot be defended, since data grabbed without a purpose has risk with no justification  ✅

> **Answer:** D

- **C.** Rights handling and consent bolted on late cost multiples of designed-in versions, so privacy belongs at scoping, not launch  ✅
