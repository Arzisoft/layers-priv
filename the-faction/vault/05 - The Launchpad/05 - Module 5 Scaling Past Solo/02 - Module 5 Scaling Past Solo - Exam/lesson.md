# Module 5: Scaling Past Solo — Exam

> Exam for **Module 5: Scaling Past Solo** (The Launchpad) — 25 questions, pass mark 80%. Correct answers are marked ✅ (read from each question's own post record; no quiz attempt was started to get them).

### Q1. What is the clearest signal that a solo builder should bring in other builders?

- **A.** Demand consistently exceeds your capacity, and you're declining good work or quality is slipping  ✅
- **B.** Reaching one year in business, since scaling is a calendar milestone rather than a demand decision
- **C.** A competitor announcing that they hired, since staying solo against a team is an unwinnable spot
- **D.** The first moment of profitability, since surplus cash should always convert into headcount fast

> **Answer:** A

### Q2. For a builder's first expansion, what does subcontracting offer that hiring an employee does not?

- **A.** Subcontractors legally cannot quit mid-project, giving your delivery commitments real enforcement teeth
- **B.** Subcontractors work without contracts, cutting the paperwork burden that slows early growth down
- **C.** Subcontractor costs are invisible to clients, while employee salaries must appear in proposals
- **D.** Capacity that flexes with the pipeline: you pay for delivery when there's work, without fixed overhead  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q3. You're no longer the one directing the AI on every build. How does quality stay consistent?

- **A.** It can't; quality is personal, which is why serious builders never let anyone else touch delivery
- **B.** Through written standards and checklists every build must pass, so quality lives in the process  ✅
- **C.** By hiring only senior people and trusting them fully, since oversight insults experienced builders
- **D.** By reviewing one random project each quarter, since sampling is the same as a full review

> **Answer:** B

### Q4. What must a subcontractor agreement cover before any client work is shared?

- **A.** Only the pay rate, since adding terms beyond compensation signals distrust and sours the whole start
- **B.** The subcontractor's personal financial situation, verifying they can survive payment delays
- **C.** Scope, pay, deadlines, confidentiality, work ownership, and whether they may approach your clients  ✅
- **D.** A guarantee of future volume, since subcontractors legally require minimum committed workloads

> **Answer:** C

### Q5. You bill a module at your standard rate and pay a subcontractor to deliver it. What must the spread cover?

- **A.** Nothing; the spread is pure profit since the subcontractor absorbed every cost of the delivery
- **B.** Only your sales commission, since finding the deal is the sole contribution that you made to it
- **C.** A legally fixed percentage, since regulations cap what intermediaries may keep on services
- **D.** Your QA, client management, revisions risk, and margin — the work of owning the outcome  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q6. What kind of work should a scaling builder delegate first?

- **A.** Well-defined, repeatable build work with clear standards, where your playbook already exists  ✅
- **B.** Sales conversations, since new prospects can't tell the difference between builders anyway
- **C.** The most ambiguous problems, since difficult work is how subcontractors prove themselves
- **D.** Client billing disputes, since conflict is the task that owners should shed before anything else

> **Answer:** A

### Q7. A subcontractor is delivering most of a client's build. Who should the client hear from?

- **A.** Only the subcontractor, since direct contact between doer and client removes all communication lag
- **B.** Nobody regularly, since scheduled updates create expectations that constrain the delivery team
- **C.** You, or a clearly designated lead, so the client has one accountable voice regardless of builder  ✅
- **D.** Both of you interchangeably at random, so the client experiences the full depth of the team

> **Answer:** C

### Q8. What makes a small team of AI Directed Engineers produce consistent output?

- **A.** Hiring people with identical backgrounds, since uniform experience always produces uniform results
- **B.** A shared playbook: the same process, standards, and review gates applied to every single build  ✅
- **C.** Competition between builders, since internal rivalry reliably raises everyone's output quality
- **D.** Keeping builders isolated from each other, since collaboration introduces inconsistent habits

> **Answer:** B

### Q9. What review gate protects your reputation once others deliver under your brand?

- **A.** Nothing ships to a client until it passes the quality bar — the standards, tests, and review you own  ✅
- **B.** Client-side review only, since the customer is the most motivated quality inspector available
- **C.** Subcontractor self-certification, since professionals accurately judge their own finished work anyway
- **D.** A one-week hold on every deliverable, since elapsed time alone surfaces most of the defects

> **Answer:** A

### Q10. How should you represent your team to clients?

- **A.** As employees of a large firm, since perceived size is what wins competitive engagements
- **B.** Not at all; conceal that anyone else exists, since clients believe they hired only you
- **C.** By introducing every subcontractor's rate, so the client understands the full cost structure
- **D.** Honestly: you lead a small team you stand behind, and you remain accountable for the result  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q11. What should carry the most weight when selecting builders to subcontract to?

- **A.** The lowest rate offered, since delivery cost is the primary lever in a services business
- **B.** Years of traditional coding experience, since AI direction skill maps directly across from seniority
- **C.** Demonstrated work and process discipline: can they ship to your standard, shown by real builds  ✅
- **D.** Personal friendship, since trust between friends outperforms any evaluation of capability

> **Answer:** C

### Q12. Sales is going well and you're tempted to sign three more builds this month. What check comes first?

- **A.** Whether you have the delivery capacity — committed builder hours — to ship it all on time and to standard  ✅
- **B.** Whether competitors are also signing that volume, since matching the market's pace is the top priority
- **C.** Nothing; sign everything and figure delivery out later, since revenue solves every problem
- **D.** Whether the projects can all share one codebase, since combined builds are cheaper to run

> **Answer:** A

### Q13. How should you handle paying subcontractors?

- **A.** After the client pays you, whenever that happens, since risk should flow down the chain naturally
- **B.** On the terms you agreed, promptly and predictably; reliable pay is how you keep reliable builders  ✅
- **C.** In merchandise or platform credits where possible, preserving cash for marketing expenses
- **D.** Annually in a lump sum, since batched payments reduce transaction fees on both sides of it

> **Answer:** B

### Q14. What responsibilities should a scaling builder keep longest?

- **A.** All production work, since the builder's personal touch is the competitive advantage
- **B.** Selling, scoping, and the final quality call: the judgment the whole business is built on  ✅
- **C.** Bookkeeping, since financial tasks are too sensitive to involve anyone else in the operation
- **D.** Social media posting, since brand voice cannot survive delegation to any other human being

> **Answer:** B

### Q15. How should a new subcontractor's first assignment be structured?

- **A.** A small, paid, low-stakes pilot with clear standards, so you learn how they work before clients depend on them  ✅
- **B.** Your largest active project, since high stakes reveal true capability faster than anything gentle
- **C.** An unpaid trial build, since free work filters out people who aren't serious about the relationship
- **D.** No assignment at all until they shadow you for a month, since observation alone builds readiness

> **Answer:** A

### Q16. Why does documentation become critical the moment a second builder touches delivery?

- **A.** It doesn't; documentation is busywork that slows real builders down and insults their intelligence
- **B.** Only for compliance reasons, since regulations require written records of every subcontractor interaction
- **C.** Because the process has to live outside your head: a builder can't deliver to your standard from a verbal description  ✅
- **D.** So that clients can audit the internal process, since transparency requires sharing all internal notes

> **Answer:** C

### Q17. A good subcontractor delivers below your quality standard on back-to-back projects. What do you diagnose first?

- **A.** Whether the scope was clear, the standard was shared, and the workload was sane, before concluding it's a people problem  ✅
- **B.** Nothing; two misses means the relationship is over, since reliable builders never slip twice in a row
- **C.** Their personal life, since performance dips always trace back to issues outside of the professional work
- **D.** Your own sales pipeline, since quality dips are always caused by accepting too many projects at once

> **Answer:** A

### Q18. A subcontractor needs access to a client's system to complete the build. What's the right approach?

- **A.** Share your own login credentials for speed, since a new account just wastes the client's time
- **B.** Client consent where required, minimum necessary access, and confidentiality in writing  ✅
- **C.** Full admin access to everything, since partial access causes integration errors that cost more later
- **D.** No access at all; the subcontractor should build locally and you transfer the work yourself

> **Answer:** B

### Q19. What infrastructure should exist before bringing on your first subcontractor?

- **A.** A written playbook, quality checklist, and subcontractor agreement: the system they'll deliver inside  ✅
- **B.** Nothing; build the infrastructure after the first project reveals what's needed through real experience
- **C.** A dedicated office, employee handbook, and HR department, since subcontractors expect structure
- **D.** Only a Slack channel, since real-time chat replaces the need for any written documentation or process

> **Answer:** A

### Q20. Your solo delivery process works because you personally compensate for its gaps. What happens when you scale it?

- **A.** The gaps disappear, since adding builders means adding people who naturally fill the holes you covered
- **B.** Nothing changes, since the process that works for one builder works identically for any number of builders
- **C.** The gaps become features, since creative workarounds are what separate great teams from average ones
- **D.** Scaling amplifies whatever process exists: the chaos multiplies with every builder you add  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q21. How should team-delivered work be priced compared to solo work?

- **A.** At a discount, since the client deserves a lower price when the original builder isn't doing all the work
- **B.** By the outcome's value, same as always: clients buy the result and accountability, not a labor markup  ✅
- **C.** At exactly the sum of all builder hourly rates, since transparency requires itemizing every person's time
- **D.** At triple the solo rate, since team coordination automatically triples the value of any delivered output

> **Answer:** B

### Q22. You now spend ten hours a week coordinating builders instead of building. Is that a problem?

- **A.** Yes, coordination is overhead that should be eliminated by hiring self-managing builders who need no direction
- **B.** Yes, and you should stop coordinating and return to production, since building is the only real work
- **C.** No. Coordination is how team output exceeds what solo hours alone could produce. It's the job now  ✅
- **D.** No, but only if you bill those hours directly to clients as a separate line item on every single invoice

> **Answer:** C

### Q23. What does the non-solicitation term in a subcontractor agreement actually protect?

- **A.** It prevents the subcontractor from ever working in the same industry again, eliminating all future competition
- **B.** It guarantees the subcontractor will never leave, since the legal penalty for departure is too severe to risk
- **C.** It has no real effect, since non-solicitation clauses are universally unenforceable in every jurisdiction
- **D.** It prevents the subcontractor from approaching your clients directly, backed by you staying genuinely valuable  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q24. When a working relationship with a subcontractor truly isn't working, what's the right exit?

- **A.** Ghost them: stop sending work and hope they figure it out on their own without an awkward conversation
- **B.** Keep them on indefinitely but reassign all their work, since firing creates legal risk that avoidance eliminates
- **C.** Part ways directly and cleanly: settle what's owed, transition the work, end it per the agreement  ✅
- **D.** Publicly criticize their work to justify the decision, since transparency with the community matters most

> **Answer:** C

### Q25. What guiding principle should drive every scaling decision an AI Directed Engineer makes?

- **A.** Maximize the number of builders quickly, since team size is the primary measure of success
- **B.** Never scale at all, since the solo model is always superior to any team configuration in every situation
- **C.** Scale only when it's cheaper than solo delivery, since cost reduction is the sole reason teams exist
- **D.** Scale on demand signal, systematize quality, and keep the judgment calls that built your reputation  ✅

> **Answer:** D
