# Module 6: Discounting, Negotiation, and Holding Your Price — Exam

> Exam for **Module 6: Discounting, Negotiation, and Holding Your Price** (The Launchpad) — 25 questions, pass mark 80%. Correct answers are marked ✅ (read from each question's own post record; no quiz attempt was started to get them).

### Q1. A builder drops 10 percent the moment a client hesitates, receiving nothing in return. What did the client just learn?

- **A.** That the builder is flexible and generous, which strengthens the working relationship
- **B.** That the posted prices are opening bids, and every future number can be pushed  ✅
- **C.** That hesitation is rude, since it visibly cost the builder margin on this deal
- **D.** That the project scope was too large, and future proposals will arrive smaller

> **Answer:** B

### Q2. What separates a legitimate discount from price erosion?

- **A.** Size: single-digit percentage cuts are hygiene while double digits are erosion
- **B.** Timing: discounts offered before the proposal are fine, after it they erode
- **C.** Frequency: any builder can discount twice a year without training the market
- **D.** Exchange: the discount is purchased with something concrete, never given free  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q3. A client wants a lower price and the builder is open to it, for the right return. Which considerations legitimately purchase a discount?

- **A.** A kind testimonial, a social media mention, and an introduction to one colleague
- **B.** Faster decision-making, fewer meetings, and a promise of clearer feedback cycles
- **C.** Case study and referral rights, full prepayment, timeline slack, or committed volume  ✅
- **D.** Reduced oversight, since less client involvement lowers the builder's true cost

> **Answer:** C

### Q4. A retainer client pays a lower effective rate than one-off clients. When is this sound pricing rather than a cave?

- **A.** When the client asked first, since responsiveness to loyal clients builds trust
- **B.** Never: any lower rate erodes the card no matter how it was arrived at
- **C.** When margins allow it that month, keeping goodwill aligned with cash flow
- **D.** When it is a designed structure with clear thresholds, published in advance  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q5. 'Can you do better on this price?' arrives by email. What is the disciplined first move?

- **A.** A modest counter, signaling flexibility while protecting most of the margin
- **B.** Silence for a day, letting the pressure of the pause work on the buyer
- **C.** A firm restatement of the price, since any engagement invites more pushing
- **D.** Ask what drives the request before countering, separating money from doubt  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q6. Diagnosis reveals a genuine budget ceiling: the client values the build but has 8,000, not 11,000. What does this situation get?

- **A.** A scope solution: phase the work or trim deliverables while the rate holds  ✅
- **B.** A proof solution: case studies and guarantees that justify finding more budget
- **C.** A price solution: meet the 8,000, since real constraints deserve real movement
- **D.** A timing solution: wait for next quarter and requote the full scope unchanged

> **Answer:** A

### Q7. Diagnosis reveals the client doubts the build will deliver the promised value. What is the correct response?

- **A.** A goodwill discount, since meeting doubt halfway keeps the conversation alive
- **B.** Proof and problem math: no number moves while trust is the actual issue  ✅
- **C.** A scope reduction, giving the doubt a smaller and safer surface to attach to
- **D.** A guarantee of satisfaction, converting the doubt into a refund obligation

> **Answer:** B

### Q8. In a scope trade, what exactly holds constant and what changes?

- **A.** The deliverables hold; the timeline stretches until the price feels earned
- **B.** The total price holds; deliverables grow until the client feels whole again
- **C.** The rate holds; the amount of work shrinks until it fits the client's spend  ✅
- **D.** The relationship holds; both price and scope flex to keep the client signed

> **Answer:** C

### Q9. A builder cuts 11,000 to 9,000 with scope unchanged, and the deal closes. What begins at that moment?

- **A.** A stronger relationship: the client remembers flexibility and refers generously
- **B.** Perpetual haggling: the client saw the padding was real and pushes each invoice  ✅
- **C.** Nothing: one-time concessions on closing deals carry no forward signal at all
- **D.** Margin recovery: the builder can quietly rebuild the 2,000 through change orders

> **Answer:** B

### Q10. Which cluster of behaviors marks a price-only buyer?

- **A.** Slow replies, long procurement cycles, and requests for references before signing
- **B.** Detailed technical questions, milestone scrutiny, and firm delivery expectations
- **C.** No interest in proof or scope, only the number; every conversation goes to price  ✅
- **D.** Enthusiasm for the roadmap, quick verbal yeses, and slow-moving paperwork after

> **Answer:** C

### Q11. Declining a deal is described in this module as a pricing decision. What makes walking away 'profitable'?

- **A.** The capacity it frees goes to full-rate work instead of high-demand low-margin work  ✅
- **B.** Declined buyers usually return within a week and accept the original number
- **C.** Walkaways generate scarcity buzz that raises what other prospects will offer
- **D.** Lost deals are tax-deductible as business development costs in most regions

> **Answer:** A

### Q12. What are the rules of a well-executed walk-away message?

- **A.** Firm and final: cite the disrespect of the lowball so the lesson lands clearly
- **B.** Open-ended: name a discount that would work, in case the buyer finds budget
- **C.** Warm and clean: the price stands, the door stays open, no counteroffer follows  ✅
- **D.** Delayed: let the thread go quiet and answer only if the buyer writes again

> **Answer:** C

### Q13. What should a builder have decided before any negotiation conversation begins?

- **A.** The maximum discount percentage they will accept if the client asks three times
- **B.** Which competitor to recommend, so walkaways stay warm and referrals flow back
- **C.** The exact closing script, since improvised language is where negotiations die
- **D.** Their floor, their tradeable scope items, and the line at which they walk away  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q14. Most 'can you do better' asks are described as tests. What does an instant price drop do with that test?

- **A.** Fails it: the drop confirms the number was soft and invites the next push  ✅
- **B.** Passes it: decisiveness is what buyers testing responsiveness want to see
- **C.** Defers it: the real test arrives later, at the contract and invoice stage
- **D.** Neutralizes it: once price moves, the buyer's attention shifts to delivery

> **Answer:** A

### Q15. Why does negotiating without a pre-computed floor reliably end badly?

- **A.** Clients sense missing preparation and escalate their demands accordingly
- **B.** In-the-moment arithmetic under social pressure always rounds downward  ✅
- **C.** Floors are legally required in most service contracts above small amounts
- **D.** Without a floor, scope trades become impossible to calculate on the call

> **Answer:** B

### Q16. A builder agrees to 15 percent off in exchange for a case study. What two conditions complete the exchange properly?

- **A.** A handshake and an invoice note, keeping the arrangement light and friendly
- **B.** Client confidentiality and builder discretion, so the discount sets no precedent
- **C.** An oral agreement and a calendar reminder to collect the case study later
- **D.** Both sides in writing, and a time-box so the deal does not become the rate  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q17. A client has a real quarterly cap below the quote. The builder proposes delivering the full scope across two quarters at the original total. What is this move?

- **A.** A stealth discount: spreading payments is a price cut measured in cash terms
- **B.** Phasing: the client's constraint is honored while the rate and total survive  ✅
- **C.** A gamble: second-quarter budgets vanish, and deferred scope rarely gets built
- **D.** Scope creep insurance: the split locks the client in for two budget cycles

> **Answer:** B

### Q18. In the booking automation story, the builder held the rate, deferred two modules, and later delivered them at full price. What is the lesson?

- **A.** Procurement bluffs: the 8,500 ultimatum would have paid 11,000 immediately
- **B.** Deferral is risky: the builder got lucky that the second quarter budget appeared
- **C.** Scope trades protect the rate, and clients learn the builder's numbers are real  ✅
- **D.** Splitting projects doubles admin cost, eating whatever the rate held onto

> **Answer:** C

### Q19. How does a builder use AI to prepare for pressure moments before they happen?

- **A.** Rehearse against AI playing hardliner, haggler, and flincher until replies are boring  ✅
- **B.** Have AI draft an apology-free discount matrix covering every percentage request
- **C.** Let AI negotiate directly with procurement by email, removing emotion entirely
- **D.** Generate personality profiles of the client's team to predict their negotiation tactics

> **Answer:** A

### Q20. When should the list of tradeable scope items for a proposal be built?

- **A.** During the negotiation, so the trades reflect what the client actually values
- **B.** After signing, once delivery reality shows which items were optional all along
- **C.** Before the call: improvising trades under live pressure leaks value every time  ✅
- **D.** Never formally: keeping trades vague preserves flexibility for the builder

> **Answer:** C

### Q21. A negotiation ends. Win or lose, what does the discipline require next?

- **A.** A debrief email to the client summarizing what was and was not agreed
- **B.** A cooling-off day before the next quote, resetting the builder's anchors
- **C.** An updated rate card reflecting whatever number the market just revealed
- **D.** A pricing journal entry: the ask, the response, the outcome, the lesson  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q22. A prospect walks over price. Six weeks later they return ready to sign at the original number. What should a builder make of this pattern?

- **A.** It is common enough to plan for: some walkaways return as full-price clients  ✅
- **B.** It is a trap: returning buyers re-negotiate after signing more than new ones
- **C.** It deserves a reward: returning buyers have earned the discount they wanted
- **D.** It is embarrassing for the client, so the builder should reopen at a lower number

> **Answer:** A

### Q23. What happens to a positioned builder who decides to win price-only buyers anyway?

- **A.** Volume compensates: price-sensitive segments are larger and churn evens out
- **B.** They become the cheap option, repelling the clients positioning was built for  ✅
- **C.** Nothing structural: segments are separate and premium clients never notice
- **D.** Margins recover later: the cheap clients upgrade once they experience the quality

> **Answer:** B

### Q24. Which phrasing correctly opens the diagnostic move after a price ask?

- **A.** 'Help me understand: is this a budget ceiling, or does the value feel off?'  ✅
- **B.** 'What number did you have in mind?' inviting the client to name the target first
- **C.** 'Our prices are firm,' establishing position before any discussion begins
- **D.** 'What are competitors quoting you?' anchoring the talk to the market range

> **Answer:** A

### Q25. Compress this module's entire negotiation discipline into one rule.

- **A.** No concession without consideration, and no move of any kind before diagnosis  ✅
- **B.** Every negotiation is winnable: persistence and warmth eventually close any gap
- **C.** Concede early and small, since tiny planned discounts prevent larger demands
- **D.** Avoid negotiation entirely: posted prices and silence do the work of scripts

> **Answer:** A
