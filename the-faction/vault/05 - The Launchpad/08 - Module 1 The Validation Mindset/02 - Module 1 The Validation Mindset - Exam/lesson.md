# Module 1: The Validation Mindset — Exam

> Exam for **Module 1: The Validation Mindset** (The Launchpad) — 25 questions, pass mark 80%. Correct answers are marked ✅ (read from each question's own post record; no quiz attempt was started to get them).

### Q1. A builder wants a CRM for dentists because his cousin complained once at Thanksgiving. He insists dentists clearly need this. What would upgrade his claim from belief to evidence?

- **A.** A detailed competitor analysis showing that no other CRM product currently targets dental practices
- **B.** A strong gut feeling backed by his cousin's complaint, since family members have no reason to mislead him about pain
- **C.** Positive comments from friends who agree that dental offices sound disorganized and could use better software tools
- **D.** Real signals from actual dentists, such as their words, behavior, money, or commitments to use what he would build  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q2. A vibebuilder can direct her AI to ship a prototype in a weekend, so she skips customer conversations entirely. According to the module, why does cheap AI building make skipping validation more tempting?

- **A.** Building feels like progress while asking questions feels like stalling, and fast builds make validation seem slow  ✅
- **B.** AI prototypes are now accurate enough to prove demand on their own, so separate validation adds little new insight these days
- **C.** Modern AI tools include analytics that reveal market demand automatically once the first version of the app ships
- **D.** Validation only mattered when building was expensive, so cheap builds mean testing ideas first is mostly optional

> **Answer:** A

### Q3. A vibebuilder is certain her mobile app will take off, so she only pitches it to friends she knows will cheer for it. What does the module say is the real fix for this trap?

- **A.** Pitch to a larger group of supporters so the sample size grows big enough to smooth out any individual politeness bias
- **B.** Care more about learning the truth than about being right, and actively seek information that could kill the idea  ✅
- **C.** Stop pitching entirely and rely on anonymous online surveys, which remove the social pressure to respond politely
- **D.** Build the app quickly so the market itself can respond, since launch data is more honest than any conversation

> **Answer:** B

### Q4. A first-time founder thinks validating her SaaS idea means writing a 40 page market report and blasting a survey to her followers. How does the module actually define validation?

- **A.** A formal research phase where you compile industry statistics and competitor data before committing to a direction
- **B.** A one-time survey of your audience that measures interest levels before you invest any serious time in a build
- **C.** A sequence of small, cheap tests, each designed to kill or confirm one specific assumption the idea depends on  ✅
- **D.** A branding exercise where you test names and landing pages until one version clearly resonates with early visitors

> **Answer:** C

### Q5. A builder lists ten assumptions behind his dental CRM idea and wants to test the easiest ones first to build momentum. Why does the module say to attack the riskiest assumption instead?

- **A.** Easy assumptions produce unreliable data, so testing them first will usually contaminate the rest of your results
- **B.** Investors expect founders to tackle hard problems first, and validation is mostly about proving your work ethic
- **C.** If the riskiest assumption fails, nothing else matters, so testing it first saves wasted effort everywhere else  ✅
- **D.** Risky assumptions get harder to test over time, so delaying them means you may never get trustworthy answers later

> **Answer:** C

### Q6. A founder spent three months building a SaaS nobody wanted. A focused validation week would have cost him about five days of attention. What does the module call this comparison?

- **A.** A sunk cost problem, where his three months of effort now makes it emotionally harder to abandon the failed product
- **B.** An opportunity tax that only applies to solo builders who lack a team to run validation and building in parallel
- **C.** A learning curve expense, since first products usually fail and the real value is the experience gained building
- **D.** A cost asymmetry where the downside of skipping a step is about 20 times larger than the cost of the step itself  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q7. A builder collects four signals for her app idea: a compliment, a story about a painful recent experience, an email signup, and a prepayment. Per the module's evidence hierarchy, which is strongest?

- **A.** The prepayment, because money is the strongest signal there is and costs the buyer something real to hand over  ✅
- **B.** The painful story, because emotional detail about the problem proves demand better than any single transaction
- **C.** The email signup, because it scales into a measurable list while payments are just one-off anecdotal data points
- **D.** The compliment, because unprompted enthusiasm is rarer and more meaningful than transactional signals like money

> **Answer:** A

### Q8. After a demo, three people tell a builder his dentist CRM sounds awesome, and he logs this as demand. Why does the module warn against counting compliments as evidence?

- **A.** Compliments only count when they come from strangers, and these three listeners already knew the builder socially
- **B.** Verbal feedback is always unreliable, so builders should ignore everything people say and track only click data
- **C.** A compliment costs the speaker nothing, so it gives you false confidence going into a build without real demand  ✅
- **D.** Three responses is too small a sample, though the same compliments from thirty people would be solid evidence

> **Answer:** C

### Q9. A builder validates his mobile app idea by asking his family and his biggest fans, and everyone loves it. What does the module say their enthusiasm actually measures?

- **A.** Their loyalty to him, not his market, because people who want to encourage you will say yes regardless of demand  ✅
- **B.** Genuine early demand, since family and fans are real people whose opinions form a legitimate starting sample
- **C.** Product quality, since even biased listeners can accurately judge whether an app concept is well designed or not overall
- **D.** Nothing at all, since the module says every conversation is worthless and only paid transactions carry meaning

> **Answer:** A

### Q10. A founder spends two weeks reading industry reports and browsing Reddit threads about his niche, then declares his SaaS idea validated. What is the module's objection?

- **A.** Research never puts the idea in front of a real customer, and validation requires direct contact with your market  ✅
- **B.** Two weeks is too short for desk research, and a proper validation phase requires at least a quarter of analysis time
- **C.** Reddit skews negative, so the honest complaints he found there will understate the real demand for his product
- **D.** Reports are fine but Reddit is not, since anonymous forum posts fail the module's standard for credible sources

> **Answer:** A

### Q11. A vibebuilder decides to have her AI build just a quick version of her app before doing any validation, reasoning it only takes a day. What danger does the module flag?

- **A.** Quick versions built by AI are usually too buggy to demo, so early users will judge the idea on broken software
- **B.** A one-day build violates the One Week Rule, which requires seven full days of building before showing anyone
- **C.** Prototypes make later pivots technically expensive because early choices lock the product into a wrong direction
- **D.** The prototype becomes the procrastination, and once it exists she is invested and filters signals to justify it  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q12. A builder is excited about a clever scheduling solution and starts testing whether users like his design before anything else. What order does the module insist on?

- **A.** Solution first, because a compelling product makes it easier to get honest answers about the underlying problem
- **B.** Problem first, always, because if the problem is not real and painful the elegance of the solution is irrelevant  ✅
- **C.** Both together, because problems and solutions can only be understood as a pair during real customer conversations
- **D.** Market first, because even a painful problem is worthless if the audience is too small to sustain the business

> **Answer:** B

### Q13. A builder runs a landing page test, gets weak results, then decides the signup bar he set was too strict and counts it as a pass anyway. Which pitfall is this?

- **A.** Moving the goalposts, because deciding what evidence counts after collecting it lets you rationalize any outcome  ✅
- **B.** Treating compliments as demand, because he is accepting weak praise as a substitute for measurable conversions
- **C.** Confusing research with validation, because a landing page is a research artifact rather than a genuine live test
- **D.** Validating the solution before the problem, because landing pages can only ever test solutions, never problems

> **Answer:** A

### Q14. A builder with a CRM idea for dentists wants to use the Assumption Dump from the module's toolkit. What does that exercise ask him to do?

- **A.** List every feature the product needs, then rank them by build effort so his AI can start with the cheapest ones
- **B.** Write down every assumption the idea depends on, then circle the one that kills the idea if it turns out false  ✅
- **C.** Paste his idea description into his AI and ask it to generate a validation report with market size estimates
- **D.** Interview five dentists and record every objection they raise, then sort those objections from mild to severe

> **Answer:** B

### Q15. A founder keeps an Evidence Ledger with columns for what she believes and what proof she has. One belief has an empty proof column. How does the module tell her to treat it?

- **A.** As a fact she can rely on for now, since the ledger exists to document evidence rather than to challenge beliefs at all
- **B.** As a task for her AI, which should search the web until it finds supporting data to fill the missing column in
- **C.** As a sign the belief is probably wrong, since true claims about customers almost always come with easy proof
- **D.** As a risk rather than a fact, because a belief with no proof behind it is exactly what validation exists to test  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q16. A builder wonders why the One Week Rule prescribes one focused week of validation before any build begins. What is the module's reasoning for that length?

- **A.** One week matches the average time investors expect between an idea and a pitch, keeping fundraising on schedule
- **B.** Any longer would let competitors copy the idea, so a week balances learning against the risk of moving too slow
- **C.** It is small enough that you will actually do it, and long enough to generate real signal from potential customers  ✅
- **D.** Seven days is the minimum needed to run a statistically significant survey across several customer segments online

> **Answer:** C

### Q17. A vibebuilder's friends all praise her app idea, so she wants a tougher critic in her corner. How does the module suggest using her AI during validation?

- **A.** Have the AI simulate customer interviews so she can skip real conversations while still gathering usable evidence
- **B.** Ask the AI to summarize positive market trends in her niche so she walks into the build with extra confidence
- **C.** Direct the AI to argue against her idea and poke holes in her evidence, because people around her will be nice  ✅
- **D.** Use the AI to draft polite survey questions that keep respondents comfortable enough to share honest reactions

> **Answer:** C

### Q18. A builder argues that since his AI can ship an app in a weekend, failing fast in the market beats slow validation. What does the module say skipping validation actually does?

- **A.** It works for consumer apps but not business tools, because companies buy software through slower formal channels
- **B.** It saves time overall, and the module concedes that fast shippers usually outlearn careful validators eventually
- **C.** It guarantees a failed launch, since the module states no product can ever succeed without evidence gathered first
- **D.** It moves the moment of truth from before the build to after the launch, where it is far more expensive to learn  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q19. A founder worries that a validation week could be wasted if her tests come back negative. Why does the module say there is no wasted outcome?

- **A.** Negative results can be published as content, which builds an audience that will support whatever she ships next
- **B.** Every result either confirms she should build, redirects her to a stronger version, or saves her from a dead end  ✅
- **C.** Validation weeks always end positive when run correctly, because well designed tests find the demand within reach
- **D.** The skills learned running tests transfer to marketing later, so the week pays off even if the data is ignored

> **Answer:** B

### Q20. The dentist CRM builder launched to crickets after three months of building. Beyond the lost calendar time, what second cost does the module say ends builder careers?

- **A.** The financial debt from AI tooling subscriptions, which keeps charging monthly long after a failed product dies
- **B.** The reputation damage of a public failure, since future customers will remember the launch that went nowhere
- **C.** The legal exposure of shipping to a regulated industry like dentistry without a proper compliance review first
- **D.** The confidence hit of launching to silence, layered on months he could have spent on an idea with real demand  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q21. A builder gathered two signals this week: several people said cool idea, and forty strangers gave their email on a waitlist page. How should she rank them per the evidence hierarchy?

- **A.** The compliments rank higher, because spoken enthusiasm reflects genuine feeling while signups are often mindless
- **B.** The signups rank higher, because cool idea is nearly worthless while handing over an email is a stronger signal  ✅
- **C.** They rank equally, because both fall short of payment and everything below money carries about the same weight
- **D.** Neither counts at all, because the hierarchy only recognizes completed payments as legitimate evidence of demand

> **Answer:** B

### Q22. The moment a builder imagines his app succeeding, something shifts in how he processes feedback. What does the module say happens in that moment?

- **A.** He becomes more objective, because visualizing success clarifies exactly which features the market will demand
- **B.** He loses motivation to build, because imagined success satisfies the same psychological reward as real progress
- **C.** His brain starts defending the idea instead of testing it, seeking confirmation and explaining away any threats  ✅
- **D.** He overestimates timelines, because excitement causes builders to promise launch dates their AI cannot deliver

> **Answer:** C

### Q23. A founder plans to validate by emailing a survey to her entire follower list in one big blast. Why does the module reject this approach as validation?

- **A.** Surveys are acceptable only after launch, when respondents can react to a working product instead of a concept
- **B.** Validation is a sequence of small targeted tests of specific assumptions, not a broadcast blast to your audience  ✅
- **C.** Surveys must be conducted in person to count, because written answers lack the emotional detail interviews give
- **D.** One blast is too few, and the module recommends at least three survey rounds spaced a week apart for reliability

> **Answer:** B

### Q24. Two builders start with equal skill. One validates every idea for a week first, the other builds immediately. Why does the module say the validator ships winners faster over time?

- **A.** Validating means building fewer wrong things, so more total effort lands on the ideas that have real demand behind them  ✅
- **B.** Validation weeks attract early adopters who later become beta testers, shortening each product's launch timeline
- **C.** Builders who validate get faster at directing their AI, since customer language sharpens their build instructions
- **D.** Immediate builders burn out within a year, while validators pace themselves and stay productive over the long run

> **Answer:** A

### Q25. Which statement best captures the governing principle of Module 1, The Validation Mindset?

- **A.** Because AI makes building cheap, validate demand with real evidence before you build, testing your riskiest assumption first  ✅
- **B.** Successful products come from deep market research, so study your industry thoroughly before approaching customers
- **C.** Speed wins in the AI era, so ship a working prototype quickly and let genuine launch results validate the idea for you
- **D.** Great founders trust their intuition, and validation exists mainly to document a decision they have already made

> **Answer:** A
