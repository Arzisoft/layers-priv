# Module 3: Project Pricing: Fixed, Hourly, and Hybrid Models — Exam

> Exam for **Module 3: Project Pricing: Fixed, Hourly, and Hybrid Models** (The Launchpad) — 25 questions, pass mark 80%. Correct answers are marked ✅ (read from each question's own post record; no quiz attempt was started to get them).

### Q1. A client happily signs a one-number quote for a defined outcome. What did they buy, and what did the builder take on?

- **A.** They bought flexibility; the builder took on hourly reporting duty for the timeline
- **B.** They bought speed; the builder took on penalty exposure if the deadline slips at all
- **C.** They bought a discount; the builder took on the margin loss that certainty demands
- **D.** They bought certainty with capped risk; the builder took on the delivery risk  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q2. Why does the hourly trap hit vibecoders harder than traditional freelancers?

- **A.** Their hours are the most compressed, so billing time devalues their fastest work most  ✅
- **B.** Their tools bill by the hour too, so costs and revenue rise together and margins flatten
- **C.** Clients distrust AI-assisted hours, and disputes eat whatever the timesheets earn
- **D.** They juggle more projects at once, and hourly billing punishes context switching

> **Answer:** A

### Q3. A project has a defined core plus a genuinely open-ended integration mess. Which structure matches the hybrid pattern?

- **A.** Hourly for everything, with the defined core billed at a premium rate for certainty
- **B.** Fixed for everything, with the mess estimated generously and padded for safety
- **C.** A fixed base for the defined scope plus a day or hourly rate for the open portion  ✅
- **D.** Two contracts with two clients' signatures, so each half can be terminated alone

> **Answer:** C

### Q4. A builder quotes fixed on a project described only as 'a portal like our competitor has.' What is this arrangement, honestly named?

- **A.** A donation: every ambiguity becomes free work, and the client controls the ambiguity  ✅
- **B.** A fair bet: vague scopes cut both ways, and builders can interpret them narrowly
- **C.** Standard practice: most projects begin vague and definition emerges during the build
- **D.** A premium opportunity: ambiguity justifies quoting double the usual fixed price

> **Answer:** A

### Q5. What must a scope document contain before a fixed price attached to it is safe?

- **A.** The full technical architecture, so the client cannot dispute implementation choices
- **B.** Hour estimates per feature, so overruns are visible and billable when they arrive
- **C.** Deliverables, exclusions, assumptions, and the change process, all in writing  ✅
- **D.** Payment terms alone, since money mechanics are what actually end up in dispute

> **Answer:** C

### Q6. A builder is setting up payments for a fixed 12,000 build. Which structure matches the discipline?

- **A.** Half on signing, half held in escrow until the client confirms total satisfaction
- **B.** Payment in full at delivery, since confidence in your own work closes deals
- **C.** Monthly invoicing across the build, smoothing revenue whatever the milestones do
- **D.** Deposit before work, payments tied to defined milestones, final against delivery  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q7. A builder inherits an abandoned codebase nobody can size until it is opened. Which pricing model fits, and why?

- **A.** Fixed, since rescue clients need cost certainty more than any other buyer does
- **B.** Hourly or day rate: when scope is unknowable, selling time is what protects you  ✅
- **C.** Hybrid, since every project deserves at least a fixed component whatever its condition
- **D.** Value-based, since rescues restore revenue whose size sets the natural price

> **Answer:** B

### Q8. What single question should decide which pricing model a given project gets?

- **A.** What the client prefers, since structure disputes kill more deals than price does
- **B.** What the builder used last time, since consistency compounds into a reputation
- **C.** How certain the scope is: definable outcomes go fixed, unknowable work goes hourly  ✅
- **D.** How large the budget is: small projects go fixed while large ones bill by time

> **Answer:** C

### Q9. Under hourly billing, a client starts questioning individual timesheet entries and asking why a task took three hours. What dynamic is this?

- **A.** A trust breakdown unique to this client, unrelated to how the deal was structured
- **B.** Healthy oversight: hourly clients auditing time is the system functioning correctly
- **C.** A signal to raise the rate, since scrutiny means the price sits below market
- **D.** The model inviting micromanagement: selling time makes time the thing inspected  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q10. Mid-project, the client asks for a 'small addition' that is genuinely outside scope. What determines whether this costs the builder money?

- **A.** The size of the addition: under a few hours, absorbing it is cheaper than friction
- **B.** Whether a written change process exists to price it, instead of a polite negotiation  ✅
- **C.** The client relationship: strong relationships absorb additions as goodwill deposits
- **D.** The pricing model: hourly builders bill it automatically while fixed builders cannot

> **Answer:** B

### Q11. A builder starts building three days before the deposit clears, to show good faith. What did this actually signal?

- **A.** Confidence: clients reward builders who bet on themselves with faster approvals
- **B.** That the invoice is negotiable: unpaid work hands the client all the leverage  ✅
- **C.** Efficiency: early starts compound the AI speed advantage into stronger margins
- **D.** Nothing: deposits are cash flow mechanics with no signaling content either way

> **Answer:** B

### Q12. A milestone payment triggers 'when the client is satisfied with the direction.' What is wrong with this trigger?

- **A.** Nothing, if the relationship is healthy; satisfaction clauses reward good work
- **B.** It is too fast: satisfaction arrives early, and payments should trail full delivery
- **C.** It underprices the milestone, since satisfaction includes value beyond the scope
- **D.** It stalls exactly when needed: vibes are not acceptance criteria you can invoke  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q13. A prospect's legacy project is too messy to quote. Instead of guessing, what should the builder offer first?

- **A.** A free audit, absorbing the mapping cost as marketing for the larger build to come
- **B.** A generous fixed quote with a 50 percent buffer priced into the number quietly
- **C.** Paid discovery: a fixed-price mapping phase whose output feeds the build quote  ✅
- **D.** A standing retainer, since messy systems generate work indefinitely once opened

> **Answer:** C

### Q14. A rescue project runs at a day rate. The builder adds a weekly cap and Friday reports. What do these two additions do?

- **A.** They convert the deal to fixed pricing in practice, since capped time is a price
- **B.** They satisfy insurance requirements that apply to open-ended engagements
- **C.** They cap the client's cost exposure and keep trust while the unknowns resolve  ✅
- **D.** They protect the builder from scope creep by documenting each week's requests

> **Answer:** C

### Q15. Three weeks into an hourly rescue, the codebase is finally mapped and the remaining work is definable. What does the discipline suggest now?

- **A.** Stay hourly to the end, since switching models mid-project reads as opportunism
- **B.** Raise the rate, since mapped work is more valuable than exploratory work was
- **C.** Pause invoicing until delivery, converting accrued hours into a success fee
- **D.** Convert the rest to a fixed quote, now that exclusions can be written honestly  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q16. A client insists on hourly for a tightly defined build the builder can deliver in four days. What is the recommended response?

- **A.** Accept it: client preference on structure outranks builder economics every time
- **B.** Decline the project: hourly demands on defined work signal a controlling client
- **C.** Model the revenue both ways, then sell the certainty fixed pricing gives the client  ✅
- **D.** Accept hourly but pad the estimate, recovering fixed-level margin through hours

> **Answer:** C

### Q17. Where in the three models does the AI speed advantage survive as builder margin?

- **A.** In well-scoped fixed projects, where compressed delivery changes nothing billed  ✅
- **B.** In hourly projects, where premium rates translate speed directly into more revenue
- **C.** In hybrids only, where the rate card portion absorbs all the efficiency gains
- **D.** Nowhere: the advantage always reaches the client as either speed or savings

> **Answer:** A

### Q18. In the day-rate-with-cap arrangement for unknowable work, who is protected, and from what?

- **A.** Both sides: the builder from unpriceable scope, the client from runaway spend  ✅
- **B.** The builder only: caps are cosmetic, and rates shift all risk to the client
- **C.** The client only: the builder still carries delivery risk on the whole engagement
- **D.** Neither: rescue work is unprotectable, and the structure is theater for comfort

> **Answer:** A

### Q19. A builder almost never quotes hourly. Why keep a current rate card anyway?

- **A.** Marketplaces require posted rates, and stale numbers filter the builder out
- **B.** Tax reporting uses standard hourly rates to classify income from irregular projects
- **C.** Clients respect published rates, and rate cards double as positioning proof
- **D.** It anchors hybrid rates and change-order pricing whenever those come into play  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q20. Beyond smoothing cash flow, what does a milestone payment structure cap?

- **A.** Scope growth, since each milestone freezes the feature list attached to it
- **B.** Exposure to nonpayment: at most one milestone of work is ever at risk unpaid  ✅
- **C.** Client anxiety, since regular invoices signal steady progress without meetings
- **D.** Tax liability, since spread payments land across more favorable periods

> **Answer:** B

### Q21. A project runs 40 percent over the estimate. Compare who absorbs that under fixed versus hourly.

- **A.** Fixed: the client, through change orders; hourly: the builder, through goodwill
- **B.** Fixed: the builder eats the overrun; hourly: the client pays for every extra hour  ✅
- **C.** Both models split overruns evenly once they cross a quarter of the original size
- **D.** Neither absorbs it: overruns void the agreement and trigger a renegotiation phase

> **Answer:** B

### Q22. A builder keeps absorbing 'tiny' additions to preserve goodwill, and the project is now 30 percent larger than scoped. What was the structural failure?

- **A.** No written change process, so each addition became a negotiation lost politely  ✅
- **B.** Underpricing at the start, since a higher price would have absorbed the additions
- **C.** Weak milestones, since payment checkpoints naturally block scope from growing
- **D.** The fixed model itself, since hourly billing makes additions self-correcting

> **Answer:** A

### Q23. A builder downloads a contract template and ships it to a client unreviewed. What is the disciplined position on this?

- **A.** Templates are sufficient: contract disputes are rare enough to accept the risk
- **B.** Templates are a start, not legal advice; language deserves professional review  ✅
- **C.** Templates are dangerous: every agreement should be drafted from scratch by counsel
- **D.** Templates are fine once AI has reviewed them against the project's specifics

> **Answer:** B

### Q24. Scope documents, exclusions lists, and change-order templates get drafted with AI on each project. What happens to these assets over time?

- **A.** They add up: each project hones a reusable library that quotes faster and safer  ✅
- **B.** They expire: scope language dates quickly and reuse invites yesterday's disputes
- **C.** They commoditize: clients recognize recycled documents and discount accordingly
- **D.** They accumulate risk: reused exclusions eventually contradict newer agreements

> **Answer:** A

### Q25. One principle sits under every structure decision in this module. Which is it?

- **A.** Structure allocates risk: choose the model by scope certainty, never by habit  ✅
- **B.** Structure follows size: small work goes fixed and large work bills by the hour
- **C.** Structure follows the client: whoever pays gets to pick the shape of the deal
- **D.** Structure is cosmetic: with a strong number, any wrapper produces the same result

> **Answer:** A
