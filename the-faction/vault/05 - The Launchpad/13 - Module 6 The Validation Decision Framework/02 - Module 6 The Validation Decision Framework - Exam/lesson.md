# Module 6: The Validation Decision Framework — Exam

> Exam for **Module 6: The Validation Decision Framework** (The Launchpad) — 25 questions, pass mark 80%. Correct answers are marked ✅ (read from each question's own post record; no quiz attempt was started to get them).

### Q1. A builder finished twelve interviews, a landing page test, and competitor research for an AI invoicing tool. She asks you how she knows when she has enough validation to decide. What do you tell her?

- **A.** Enough validation means hitting a fixed number, like ten positive interviews, before any go decision is defensible.
- **B.** There is no magic number; look for converging evidence, multiple independent signals all pointing the same direction.  ✅
- **C.** Keep gathering data until you feel certain, because deciding under uncertainty is how builders end up shipping the wrong thing.
- **D.** Enough validation is reached once the market sizing shows a large enough total market to justify building anything.

> **Answer:** B

### Q2. You are scoring an AI meal-planning idea on the validation scorecard before a go/no-go call. Which five dimensions does the scorecard ask you to score from 1 to 5?

- **A.** Team strength, funding runway, product roadmap, launch timing, and pricing power, each judged against your chosen niche.
- **B.** Problem evidence, revenue potential, technical feasibility, branding strength, and distribution reach for the idea.
- **C.** Customer interviews, waitlist size, ad conversion rate, churn estimate, and projected margins across the first year.
- **D.** Problem evidence, solution signal, market size, competitive position, and your unfair advantage as the builder.  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q3. Fifteen people told a builder 'I would totally use that' about his AI contract summarizer, but nobody preordered or joined his waitlist organically. He wants to call it a go. What is the flaw?

- **A.** Enthusiasm is not commitment; a go requires at least one costly signal like a preorder, signed LOI, or organic waitlist growth.  ✅
- **B.** The sample is simply too small; he needs at least fifty verbal endorsements before enthusiasm becomes statistically valid evidence.
- **C.** He asked the wrong audience; positive quotes only count when they come from buyers inside a large and fast-growing target market.
- **D.** He skipped the pre-mortem step, which is the only tool that converts spoken enthusiasm into a scoreable commitment signal.

> **Answer:** A

### Q4. Eight of twelve dentists told a builder that scheduling, not contact management, was their real pain. She had already named her CRM and sketched its dashboard. What did the framework call for at that moment?

- **A.** Kill the idea outright, since the interviews proved the dental software market has no real pain worth building for.
- **B.** Build the CRM anyway but budget more for marketing, because a named product with a dashboard deserves a fair launch test.
- **C.** Run another full round of interviews with different dentists until contact management finally shows up as the top pain.
- **D.** Pivot to scheduling, because the problem evidence was real even though her chosen solution missed what dentists needed.  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q5. After a full validation cycle, a builder finds nobody actually has the pain, nobody will pay, and the niche is tiny. He feels killing the idea would mean he failed. How does the framework frame this?

- **A.** It is a pivot situation; he should keep the product concept and simply reposition it toward a larger adjacent market segment.
- **B.** It is a failure of research method; evidence this uniformly negative usually means the interview questions were badly designed.
- **C.** Kill the idea; when the problem itself fails, walking away is not failure, it is the entire point of validating before building.  ✅
- **D.** Pause the idea for six months or so, since markets shift quickly and today's weak demand often becomes tomorrow's missed window.

> **Answer:** C

### Q6. A builder's scorecard is strong except for one weak dimension, competitive position, which she could test cheaply in under two weeks. She is unsure whether to build or keep validating. What does the framework advise?

- **A.** Start building immediately, because a single weak dimension never justifies delaying an otherwise strong validated idea.
- **B.** Kill the idea, since any weak dimension on the scorecard signals a fatal flaw that more testing cannot realistically fix.
- **C.** Validate more; when one weak dimension can be tested cheaply in under two weeks, run that test before you commit to build.  ✅
- **D.** Average her five scores, and proceed to build only if the mean stays above three once the weak dimension is included.

> **Answer:** C

### Q7. A builder has run three validation rounds on his AI onboarding tool. Any further testing would now cost more time and money than a scrappy MVP would. What does the framework say he should do?

- **A.** Run a fourth, cheaper validation round anyway, because gathering more evidence always beats shipping something incomplete to users.
- **B.** Start building the small MVP, because at this point the fastest way to learn is to ship and watch what people do with it.  ✅
- **C.** Wait for a costly commitment signal to appear on its own before spending anything on either validation or an MVP build.
- **D.** Rescore the validation scorecard weekly until every one of the five dimensions reaches at least a four out of five.

> **Answer:** B

### Q8. 'I have already spent two months and hundreds of prompts on this build, I cannot quit now,' a builder tells you, even though her evidence points to kill. Which trap is she in, and why?

- **A.** Sunk cost bias; the months already spent are gone either way and should carry no weight in the go, pivot, or kill decision.  ✅
- **B.** Confirmation bias; she is treating her own heavy invested effort as positive evidence that customers surely want the product built.
- **C.** Shiny object syndrome; the finished build now looks more attractive to her than the unglamorous work of real validation.
- **D.** Loss aversion; she should recover her investment by launching quickly and selling hard to whoever shows up in month one.

> **Answer:** A

### Q9. Reviewing his notes, a builder keeps rereading the three glowing interviews and skimming past the nine that flagged serious objections. What trap is this, and what is the counter?

- **A.** Confirmation bias; the counter is weighing all the evidence, giving the nine negative interviews their full honest weight.  ✅
- **B.** Sunk cost bias; the counter is discounting older interviews and rerunning the full set with a fresh, neutral question script.
- **C.** Shiny object syndrome; the counter is limiting himself to one idea at a time until its scorecard is completely filled in.
- **D.** Survivorship bias; the counter is interviewing only churned prospects, since happy voices always drown out the doubters.

> **Answer:** A

### Q10. Midway through validating a bookkeeping tool with decent early signals, a builder feels a pull toward a brand new AI travel app that 'just feels easier.' Which trap is at work here?

- **A.** Sunk cost bias; his earlier bookkeeping research is anchoring him and blocking a rational move to the stronger new idea.
- **B.** Shiny object syndrome; the new idea's emotional pull is replacing evidence, tempting him to abandon a half-validated idea.  ✅
- **C.** Confirmation bias; he is seeking out travel app data that flatters the new concept while ignoring its obvious weaknesses.
- **D.** Analysis paralysis; holding two ideas at once means he should immediately build both as MVPs and let the market decide.

> **Answer:** B

### Q11. A builder is about to review her landing page results and interview notes for a final go/pivot/kill call. When should she have written her kill criteria list, and why?

- **A.** After reviewing the evidence, so the criteria can be tailored precisely to whatever the strongest signals turn out to be.
- **B.** Only if the early signals look negative, since writing exit conditions for a promising idea just manufactures unnecessary doubt.
- **C.** Before reviewing any evidence, because deciding exit conditions in advance is the strongest defense against sunk cost bias.  ✅
- **D.** After the MVP ships, because kill criteria only become meaningful once real usage data replaces validation-stage signals.

> **Answer:** C

### Q12. Before deciding on her AI tutoring idea, a builder writes: 'It is six months from now and this failed. Here is why.' What is the pre-mortem actually testing?

- **A.** Whether the failure reason she writes is something her evidence already flagged; if it is, she should believe the evidence.  ✅
- **B.** Whether she can emotionally handle failure, which predicts if she has the founder resilience to survive a rough launch.
- **C.** Whether the idea can survive competitor attacks, since imagined failure stories usually center on stronger funded rivals arriving.
- **D.** Whether her writing skills are sharp enough to craft the investor narrative she will need if the launch underperforms.

> **Answer:** A

### Q13. A builder asks why the framework tells her to direct her AI to argue against her go decision using her own evidence. What is the reasoning behind that tool?

- **A.** The AI can access broader market data than she can, so its verdict is statistically more reliable than her own scorecard.
- **B.** The AI has no emotional attachment to her idea, which makes it the cheapest honest advisor for stress testing a decision.  ✅
- **C.** Arguing with an AI trains her pitch, so the exercise is really rehearsal for defending the idea to early paying customers.
- **D.** The AI will always find a fatal flaw, so surviving its critique proves an idea is strong enough to justify building now.

> **Answer:** B

### Q14. A builder totals his validation scorecard: 14 of 25, including a 1 on problem evidence but a 5 on unfair advantage. He argues his builder edge makes it a go. What does the framework say?

- **A.** It is a go; a 5 in unfair advantage shows he can outbuild rivals, which offsets weak problem evidence in the near term.
- **B.** It is a borderline go; anything above 12 total qualifies as long as at least one dimension reaches a perfect five score.
- **C.** Not a go; under 15 total, or any 1 in problem evidence, rules it out, because problem evidence is the foundation score.  ✅
- **D.** It is a pivot; a low total with one strong dimension always means the solution should shift toward the strongest score.

> **Answer:** C

### Q15. A builder averages her five scorecard scores and notes the mean looks healthy despite a 1 in problem evidence. What pitfall is she committing?

- **A.** Endless validation looping; recalculating averages is a stalling ritual that delays the genuinely open go or kill call.
- **B.** Counting compliments as evidence; averaged scores smuggle polite enthusiasm into what should be commitment data only.
- **C.** Shiny object syndrome; a healthy-looking average tempts her to chase the next idea before finishing this evaluation.
- **D.** Averaging away a fatal flaw; a strong score elsewhere cannot rescue a 1 in problem evidence, which is the foundation.  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q16. A builder's scorecard for his AI resume tool leans heavily on praise from friends, family, and polite strangers who all called the idea great. How should he treat that input?

- **A.** Count it at half weight, since warm-audience praise is weaker than stranger praise but still directionally useful data.
- **B.** Count it fully in problem evidence, because people close to him understand his skills and judge the idea most accurately.
- **C.** Use it for solution signal only, since compliments speak to the fix he chose rather than to the underlying pain itself.
- **D.** Exclude it from the scorecard; only costly commitments belong there, because polite people will call any idea great.  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q17. A builder is launching her fourth round of customer interviews instead of shipping anything. Her data has been consistent for weeks. What does the framework suggest is really going on?

- **A.** She is likely afraid of the answer, using more validation as socially acceptable procrastination rather than needing data.  ✅
- **B.** She is being properly rigorous; four rounds is the documented minimum before any evidence can be called truly converging.
- **C.** Her interview script is flawed; consistent results across several rounds usually signal leading questions rather than real demand.
- **D.** She has sunk cost bias; the earlier interview rounds are pushing her to keep investing in the same research method again.

> **Answer:** A

### Q18. After weak solution signals on his B2B invoicing tool, a builder announces a 'pivot' to an unrelated mobile fitness game because it feels fresh. How does the framework classify this move?

- **A.** A textbook pivot; changing markets and products at once is the boldest and often the fastest route to product-market fit.
- **B.** Not a real pivot; a true pivot keeps the validated learning, and this jump is shiny object syndrome in a pivot costume.  ✅
- **C.** A justified kill and restart, since weak solution signals always invalidate everything learned about the original market.
- **D.** A reasonable hedge; running the game alongside the invoicing tool lets the market pick the winner without a hard call.

> **Answer:** B

### Q19. A builder decides to kill his idea after the evidence fails and quietly moves on without writing anything down. What does the framework say he is leaving behind?

- **A.** The written paragraph explaining why it died, which is the most valuable asset the dead idea could have produced for him.  ✅
- **B.** The waitlist he built, which should always be transferred over to the next idea before any kill decision ever becomes official.
- **C.** Momentum; the framework says a kill must be followed within one week by scoring a replacement idea to stay sharp.
- **D.** Nothing important; a clean quiet kill is exactly what the framework prescribes once the problem evidence has failed.

> **Answer:** A

### Q20. A builder keeps delaying her go decision on an AI scheduling assistant, saying she will commit once she is certain the idea will work. What does the framework tell her about certainty?

- **A.** Certainty arrives once all five scorecard dimensions hit five, so she should keep validating until the rubric maxes out.
- **B.** Certainty comes from a successful MVP, so she should skip the decision framework and let launch results decide for her.
- **C.** Certainty is never available; the practical standard is a structured weighing of converging evidence she can defend.  ✅
- **D.** Certainty is a funding question; with enough budget for iteration, the initial go decision barely matters in the end.

> **Answer:** C

### Q21. A builder has strong problem evidence for her AI proposal writer: unprompted pain in ten separate interviews. Before she can call it a go, what else does the framework require?

- **A.** At least one costly commitment signal, such as a preorder, a signed letter of intent, or a waitlist grown without paid ads.  ✅
- **B.** A competitive analysis proving no rival exists, since any incumbent in the space converts a probable go into a forced kill.
- **C.** A finished brand identity and name, because commitment signals cannot be collected credibly for an unnamed product idea.
- **D.** Five more interview rounds, because problem evidence only counts once it comes from at least fifty separate conversations.

> **Answer:** A

### Q22. Reviewing his kill-leaning evidence, a builder feels the tug of 'but I already spent so long on this.' According to the module, what is his most immediate, practical counter to the trap?

- **A.** Rescore the scorecard from scratch, since fresh numbers automatically strip any emotional residue from the decision process.
- **B.** Take a two-week break from the idea, because distance reliably dissolves attachment better than any structured exercise.
- **C.** Name the trap out loud as sunk cost bias, because naming a decision trap strips away most of the power it holds over him.  ✅
- **D.** Ask three fellow builders to vote on the decision, because outside majorities are immune to attachment-driven reasoning.

> **Answer:** C

### Q23. A builder admits privately that he will build his AI podcast tool no matter what his validation results show, but he still runs interviews and a landing page. What pitfall is this?

- **A.** Endless validation looping; running tests without a deadline turns evidence gathering into a permanent delay mechanism.
- **B.** Treating validation as a formality; if the decision is not genuinely open, he performed a ritual, not real validation.  ✅
- **C.** Confirmation bias; predeciding guarantees he will unconsciously design tests that can only produce positive signals.
- **D.** Shiny object syndrome; commitment this strong means he is avoiding a harder, better idea sitting on his backlog.

> **Answer:** B

### Q24. A builder's interviews strongly confirm the pain, but her landing page barely converts and competitors look crowded. The signals point in different directions. What does the framework tell her to do first?

- **A.** Trust the interviews, since live human conversation always outranks passive web metrics when the two sources disagree.
- **B.** Trust the landing page, since behavioral data always outranks anything prospects merely said in a friendly interview.
- **C.** Average the signals into a single score, letting the scorecard math resolve the conflict she cannot resolve herself.
- **D.** Understand why the signals conflict before deciding, because converging evidence, not a mixed bag, is the standard.  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q25. Which statement best captures the governing principle of Module 6, The Validation Decision Framework?

- **A.** Validation succeeds when a large enough market is proven, because sizing outweighs every other signal at decision time.
- **B.** The goal of validation is certainty; a disciplined builder keeps testing until the evidence removes all remaining doubt.
- **C.** Speed is the deciding advantage; because AI lets you build in weeks, shipping fast beats deliberating over frameworks.
- **D.** Turn converging evidence into a defensible go, pivot, or kill decision with a structured framework, not with emotion.  ✅

> **Answer:** D
