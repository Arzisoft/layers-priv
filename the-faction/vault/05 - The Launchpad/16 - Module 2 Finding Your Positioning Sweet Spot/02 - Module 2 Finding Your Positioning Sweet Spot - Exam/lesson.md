# Module 2: Finding Your Positioning Sweet Spot — Exam

> Exam for **Module 2: Finding Your Positioning Sweet Spot** (The Launchpad) — 25 questions, pass mark 80%. Correct answers are marked ✅ (read from each question's own post record; no quiz attempt was started to get them).

### Q1. A builder is weighing a candidate niche and wants a quick gut check before deeper research. Which three questions cover the full intersection?

- **A.** Is the market growing, are competitors weak, and can I reach buyers cheaply through ads
- **B.** Can I build it, will these people pay for it and will I still want this work in a year  ✅
- **C.** Do I know the industry, do I have a contact inside it, and is there a conference for it
- **D.** Is the tech mature, is the niche recession-proof, and can the work be productized later

> **Answer:** B

### Q2. A builder lands in a niche with strong skills fit and eager buyers, but privately dreads every project in it. What does this module predict for month six?

- **A.** The dread fades: competence grows with repetition, and enjoyment follows competence
- **B.** The business scales anyway, since revenue and skill are the only load-bearing circles
- **C.** Clients notice nothing, because professional delivery masks personal disinterest
- **D.** Burnout: skills plus demand without enjoyment is a treadmill that stops being worth running  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q3. Another builder adores a niche and can build everything it needs, but months of effort produce no one willing to pay. What has this builder actually got?

- **A.** A hobby: skills plus enjoyment without demand is a passion project wearing a business name  ✅
- **B.** A timing problem: demand exists but lags eighteen months behind genuine innovation
- **C.** A marketing gap: paying buyers exist out there and simply have not encountered the offer yet
- **D.** A pricing error: demand appears whenever a price finally drops far enough to test it

> **Answer:** A

### Q4. A builder spots hungry buyers in a niche they would love to serve, but has never built anything the niche needs. What is the honest status of this position?

- **A.** Ideal: skills are the easiest circle to fake while quietly learning on the paying client's dime
- **B.** Blocked permanently: skills take years to build, and the demand window will close first
- **C.** A promise they cannot keep yet: the gap is closable, but selling before closing it is risk  ✅
- **D.** Irrelevant: with AI directing the build, personal skill no longer forms a real circle

> **Answer:** C

### Q5. Starting the demand analysis, a builder opens a blank research document. The mentor slides over the validation results from the prior course instead. Why?

- **A.** Blank-page research is faster, but the validation file protects against optimism bias
- **B.** The demand circle already has data: who responded and who paid is evidence in hand  ✅
- **C.** Validation results expire in weeks, so they should be checked before new research
- **D.** The prior course requires reuse of its artifacts for continuity across the tier

> **Answer:** B

### Q6. A builder chooses to lead with: I serve independent pharmacies. What does this axis buy, according to the module?

- **A.** Insider trust: knowing the niche's software, rules, and rhythms before the first call  ✅
- **B.** Urgency: industry positioning makes the problem feel expensive to leave unsolved
- **C.** Ecosystem demand: the industry funnels its buyers through shared marketplaces
- **D.** Price immunity: vertical positioning removes budget conversations entirely

> **Answer:** A

### Q7. A different builder leads with: I fix no-show appointment losses. What does problem positioning buy that the other axes do not?

- **A.** Stability: problems change more slowly than industries or platforms ever do
- **B.** Reach: problem statements translate across languages and markets without edits
- **C.** Loyalty: clients bond with whoever names their industry, not their symptom
- **D.** Urgency: a named, bleeding problem carries its own reason to act now  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q8. A builder positions entirely as the Airtable expert for small logistics firms. The module flags one structural risk unique to this axis. Which one?

- **A.** Airtable expertise is far too common now, so the position cannot differentiate anyone
- **B.** Logistics firms distrust third-party platforms and prefer custom-built software
- **C.** Platform knowledge decays faster than industry knowledge and needs constant, costly relearning
- **D.** The position is chained to a vendor's roadmap: one pivot by Airtable rewrites the business  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q9. A builder can combine two positioning axes and asks which pairing the module recommends for most builders. What is the answer?

- **A.** Platform plus industry, since tools and verticals attract two separate buyer streams
- **B.** Problem plus platform, since urgency plus ecosystem covers both motivation and reach
- **C.** Industry plus problem: insider trust and urgency, the strongest pairing for most builders  ✅
- **D.** Any two work equally well, since pairing itself is what produces the advantage

> **Answer:** C

### Q10. A builder writes: I build intake systems for private clinics so front desks stop drowning. The module calls this sentence something specific. What?

- **A.** A hypothesis: a testable claim about who will respond, awaiting evidence from real prospects  ✅
- **B.** A tagline: finished marketing copy that is ready for the website and business cards
- **C.** A contract: a scope commitment that future clients can hold the builder to at delivery
- **D.** A mission statement: the fixed, long-term identity that the business will eventually grow into

> **Answer:** A

### Q11. During a test conversation, a prospect hears the positioning statement and immediately asks: could that work for a practice with two locations? What just happened?

- **A.** A scope negotiation began, which means the statement promised too much implementation detail
- **B.** A lean-in: the prospect applied the position to their own situation, which is the signal to log  ✅
- **C.** A polite deflection: questions about odd edge cases are how prospects politely exit conversations
- **D.** A referral: multi-location questions usually signal the prospect is asking for someone else

> **Answer:** B

### Q12. After twenty conversations, prospects still respond to a builder's position with: interesting, so what do you focus on? What is the diagnosis?

- **A.** Too narrow: the position is so specific that nobody identifies with it anymore
- **B.** Just right: focus questions show prospects want to buy more than is on offer
- **C.** Too broad: the position is not landing as a specialty, so people keep asking for one  ✅
- **D.** Wrong audience: the right position is being tested on people outside the niche

> **Answer:** C

### Q13. A builder positions for a market of roughly forty buyers who all attend the same annual meetup and share vendor stories. What risk does the module name here?

- **A.** Too narrow: the pipeline cannot survive losses, and one bad project can poison the whole pool  ✅
- **B.** Concentration pricing: tight-knit communities negotiate collectively and steadily compress margins
- **C.** No risk: forty well-networked buyers is the textbook definition of a beachhead market
- **D.** Referral saturation: small markets exhaust their word of mouth within the first year

> **Answer:** A

### Q14. The module describes a position that is just right. Which pair of conditions defines it?

- **A.** No competitors exist yet, and the technology required is under a year old
- **B.** The niche is prestigious enough to mention, and its buyers have large budgets
- **C.** The builder has ten years of experience in it, and the audience trusts credentials
- **D.** Prospects instantly self-recognize, and the audience is big enough to lose a few  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q15. A builder starts the sweet-spot exercise. Per the module, which three lists get made before AI is asked to cross-reference anything?

- **A.** Competitor names, price benchmarks, and a ranked list of industry conferences
- **B.** Shipped projects, validation responders with real intent, and honest enjoyment notes  ✅
- **C.** Dream clients, revenue targets, and the skills the builder intends to learn next
- **D.** Market sizes, growth rates, and the platforms each candidate niche depends on

> **Answer:** B

### Q16. Three candidate statements are drafted and the builder wants to pick one tonight by instinct. What does the module require before choosing?

- **A.** A week of reflection, since instinct improves once the options stop feeling urgent
- **B.** Ten real conversations testing the statements, with reactions logged and compared  ✅
- **C.** A survey of fifty strangers, since volume beats depth when comparing options
- **D.** A domain purchase for each, testing which name attracts organic search traffic

> **Answer:** B

### Q17. A builder claims to enjoy all their past niches equally and cannot rank them. Where does the module say the truth is already written down?

- **A.** In revenue records: enjoyment and profit track together closely enough to substitute
- **B.** In client testimonials: happy clients reliably indicate work the builder loved
- **C.** In their own project notes: what they delayed starting tells them what they dreaded  ✅
- **D.** In hours logged: people spend more time on work they enjoy, so totals reveal it

> **Answer:** C

### Q18. A podcast convinces a builder that dental tech is lucrative, so they pick it despite no dental projects, no dental contacts, and no interest. Which mistake is this, exactly?

- **A.** Choosing by market size alone, with two of the three circles missing at the start  ✅
- **B.** Platform dependence, since dental software locks builders into vendor ecosystems
- **C.** Undertesting, since one podcast is a sample size too small for niche selection
- **D.** Overcorrection, since lucrative niches attract competitors who compress prices

> **Answer:** A

### Q19. A builder tests their positioning statement by asking three friends whether they like it. All three approve. What does the module say about this evidence?

- **A.** It counts if the friends run businesses, since owners evaluate positioning like buyers
- **B.** It is a useful first filter that should precede any testing on real prospects
- **C.** It is weakly positive: friends catch wording problems even when they cannot judge demand
- **D.** It is nearly worthless: approval from friends is not the behavior of a real prospect  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q20. Two niches look equally viable on paper, but one already produced paid interest during validation. How does the module weight the tie?

- **A.** Toward a validated one: evidence of real money beats symmetry on paper  ✅
- **B.** Toward the unvalidated one, since fresh markets hide the larger upside
- **C.** Split effort between both for a quarter and let revenue break the tie
- **D.** Toward whichever has the larger total market, since ceilings matter most

> **Answer:** A

### Q21. A builder directs AI through the whole module: cross-referencing lists, proposing candidates, drafting statements. Which step does the module reserve for the builder alone?

- **A.** Formatting the final statement, since AI drafts often read as generic to human buyers
- **B.** Counting the audience size, since AI estimates require human spot-checking
- **C.** Judging the intersection and committing to it: evidence informs, the builder decides  ✅
- **D.** Scheduling the test conversations, since calendars require human coordination

> **Answer:** C

### Q22. Before committing, a builder wants to verify a niche's demand exists beyond their own validation sample. Where does the module send them?

- **A.** Industry analyst reports, which quantify niche software spending in detail on an annual basis
- **B.** Where the buyers gather: niche communities, groups, and boards where the pain is discussed  ✅
- **C.** Competitor pricing pages, since published prices prove that someone is paying them
- **D.** Search volume tools, since query counts map fairly directly onto purchase intent

> **Answer:** B

### Q23. A builder's notes show property management conversations ran long because he was having fun, while dental calls drained him. His shipped work fits both. How should this evidence weigh in?

- **A.** The energy difference is the sustainability circle speaking, and it should count in the decision  ✅
- **B.** Feelings about conversations are noise, and only the hard validation payment data should be counted
- **C.** Dental should win anyway if its market is larger, since enjoyment tends to follow success
- **D.** He should alternate between the niches by quarter until the energy difference resolves itself

> **Answer:** A

### Q24. A builder insists on platform positioning around a fast-growing tool. Under what condition does the module accept this choice?

- **A.** If the platform is profitable, since vendor health guarantees ecosystem stability
- **B.** If the builder is certified by the vendor, which insulates against roadmap changes
- **C.** If the niche is small, since platform risk only materializes at larger scales
- **D.** With an exit plan: a way to carry the practice elsewhere when the vendor pivots  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q25. One principle governs this module's entire method, from the three lists to the ten conversations. Which statement is it?

- **A.** The best niche is the largest one the builder can plausibly claim expertise in
- **B.** Positioning is pure intuition: the right niche feels correct well before any evidence arrives
- **C.** The sweet spot is located with evidence, not chosen by ambition or a podcast's enthusiasm  ✅
- **D.** Any niche works if the builder commits hard enough, so speed of choice matters most

> **Answer:** C
