# Module 2: Problem Validation — Exam

> Exam for **Module 2: Problem Validation** (The Launchpad) — 25 questions, pass mark 80%. Correct answers are marked ✅ (read from each question's own post record; no quiz attempt was started to get them).

### Q1. A builder wants to launch an invoicing helper for freelance photographers. Before directing her AI to build anything, she checks whether the problem is worth solving. According to the module, what are the three markers of a problem worth building for?

- **A.** The problem is trendy in startup circles, simple to explain in one sentence, and cheap for a vibecoder to build for
- **B.** The problem affects a massive total market, has no existing competitors, and can be shipped with a weekend AI build
- **C.** The problem is one the builder has personally felt, sounds exciting to friends, and fits a familiar app category
- **D.** The problem is frequent, genuinely painful, and people are already working around it with some duct-tape solution  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q2. A vibebuilder polls twelve friends about his meal prep app idea and every single one says they love it. According to the module, why does this round of feedback prove almost nothing about real demand?

- **A.** Friends want him to succeed and optimize for his feelings, so their enthusiasm is affection rather than market data  ✅
- **B.** Twelve people is too small a sample, so he should keep polling friends until he has at least fifty total responses
- **C.** His friends only heard the idea described, so he should show them a working prototype before trusting the reaction
- **D.** His friends sit in the wrong age bracket, so he should recruit friends who better match his target demographics

> **Answer:** A

### Q3. Midway through a problem interview about scheduling headaches, a builder gets excited and describes the AI built booking tool she is planning. The interviewee says it sounds great. What just happened to the interview?

- **A.** She gained an early sales lead, since describing the tool let her test messaging while still gathering problem data
- **B.** She saved time by combining problem and solution validation, which works when the interviewee seems truly engaged
- **C.** Nothing changed, because the interviewee had already described genuine pain before the tool entered the conversation
- **D.** The interview died as evidence, because once she pitched, the person switched from telling the truth to being nice  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q4. A builder asks gym goers, 'Would you use an app that tracks your workout progress?' and every person says yes. Following the module, what should he ask instead?

- **A.** A pricing question, such as how much they would be willing to pay per month for the tracker once it finally launches
- **B.** A question anchored in specific past behavior, such as what they actually did last time they tried to track a workout  ✅
- **C.** A comparison question, such as which of three competing tracking apps they would be most likely to download first
- **D.** A commitment question, such as whether they would promise to sign up for the beta on the day it becomes available

> **Answer:** B

### Q5. In an interview, a prospect calls a problem 'kind of annoying' but cannot name a single thing she currently does about it. How should the builder classify this problem?

- **A.** As a nice-to-have, because genuine need-to-have problems come with existing workarounds people are already using  ✅
- **B.** As a need-to-have, because the prospect clearly recognized the pain as soon as the builder raised the topic with her
- **C.** As an open opportunity, because no workaround means no competition and a clean first mover advantage for his build
- **D.** As inconclusive, because workarounds mainly matter for business buyers and rarely apply to consumer issues

> **Answer:** A

### Q6. Three separate interviewees tell a builder 'that is such a cool idea, I love it,' and he logs each response as positive validation. What does the module say about this evidence?

- **A.** Three enthusiastic reactions from target customers is a meaningful early signal that his problem hypothesis is on track
- **B.** Compliments are rejections wearing a smile; they cost the speaker nothing, which is exactly what they are worth  ✅
- **C.** Compliments count as weak positive evidence that can be averaged together with behavioral data in his evidence log
- **D.** Compliments matter only if the interviewees were strangers, so he should first check how he sourced those three people

> **Answer:** B

### Q7. A builder just finished a problem interview with a dental office manager and opens her evidence log. According to the module, what belongs in that row?

- **A.** Her overall impression of the meeting, a score out of ten, and her own summary of what the manager probably meant to say
- **B.** Only the answers supporting her hypothesis, keeping the log focused on usable signal instead of discouraging noise
- **C.** Who she talked to, the problem in their words, frequency, workaround, cost, and a direct quote from the conversation  ✅
- **D.** The manager's contact details and a note on buying timeline, since the log doubles as an early stage sales pipeline

> **Answer:** C

### Q8. A builder compares three prospects: one says he would definitely use the tool, one shares the idea excitedly with a colleague, and one pays an assistant 300 dollars a month to handle the task manually. Which is the strongest demand signal?

- **A.** The one who says he would definitely use it, because a direct statement of intent is the clearest expression of demand
- **B.** The excited sharer, because word of mouth energy is what actually drives early adoption for brand new products
- **C.** The one paying for a manual workaround, because money already spent on a duct-tape solution is the strongest signal  ✅
- **D.** All three equally, because every positive response from a target customer deserves the same weight in the log

> **Answer:** C

### Q9. A builder surveys his list with 'Would you pay 10 dollars a month for this?' and 80 percent say yes. He launches and almost nobody buys. How does the module explain the gap?

- **A.** Future intentions are the least reliable validation data; people mispredict their behavior but rarely misremember paying  ✅
- **B.** The price point was wrong; hypothetical questions work fine when the stated price matches what the market will bear
- **C.** The sample was too small; hypothetical answers become dependable once enough survey responses have been collected
- **D.** He surveyed too early; intent questions only become accurate after people have seen a working version of the product

> **Answer:** A

### Q10. A Faction builder planned a CRM for dentists, then interviewed twelve dental office managers. They were fine with their practice software but drowning in insurance verification. What is the lesson?

- **A.** The real problem often sits right next to the imagined one, and only real conversations with the market reveal it  ✅
- **B.** Dentists were the wrong niche, and he should have picked a market where his original CRM concept would have landed
- **C.** Twelve interviews was too few to abandon the CRM; he should have kept interviewing until someone confirmed the idea
- **D.** He should have pitched the CRM harder, since office managers often undervalue tools they have never seen demoed

> **Answer:** A

### Q11. A builder validating a tool for wedding planners has interviewed her roommate and two coworkers, all supportive. What does the module direct her to do next?

- **A.** Ask the roommate and coworkers for introductions to their friends, since warm referrals respond more honestly
- **B.** Run a broad social media poll first, so she can gather hundreds of quick datapoints before slower interviews
- **C.** Keep interviewing people she knows but ask tougher questions, since question quality matters more than sourcing
- **D.** Build a list of where wedding planners actually gather, like trade forums, and interview strangers from there  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q12. After five interviews, a builder sits down with his red flag checklist as the module instructs. Which set of findings should disqualify the problem?

- **A.** Low frequency, no workaround, no budget, cool idea responses, and pain he had to explain before anyone recognized it  ✅
- **B.** Small market size, strong competitors, high build complexity, long sales cycles, and costly customer acquisition paths
- **C.** Interviewees who asked few questions, short meetings, flat body language, and reluctance to schedule any follow ups
- **D.** Conflicting feature requests, unclear pricing feedback, and prospects comparing the idea to products already available

> **Answer:** A

### Q13. A builder spends five minutes explaining a productivity problem before the interviewee finally nods and agrees it sounds frustrating. How should he read this moment?

- **A.** As a win: the interviewee confirmed the problem was frustrating, which is what problem interviews exist to surface
- **B.** As a delivery issue: he should refine and shorten his explanation so future interviewees grasp the problem faster
- **C.** As an education gap: the market has the problem but lacks awareness, which good launch marketing can correct later
- **D.** As a red flag: if the person did not recognize the pain until it was described, they do not actually have the problem  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q14. A builder wraps three problem interviews and decides to write up his notes from memory at the end of the week. What risk does the module warn about?

- **A.** Memory smooths over discouraging answers and amplifies flattering ones, so he ends up validating hopes, not the market  ✅
- **B.** He may forget names and contact details, making it hard to schedule the follow up interviews Module 3 will require
- **C.** The interviews will blur together, so he should have recorded audio of every session and transcribed them at the end
- **D.** Waiting is acceptable if his eventual notes are detailed, since the core substance of an interview rarely changes much

> **Answer:** A

### Q15. A vibebuilder argues that since her AI can build the whole product in a weekend, it is faster to just ship it and see than to spend a week interviewing. What is the module's response?

- **A.** She is right; when building costs only a weekend, shipping the real product is the fastest and cheapest validation
- **B.** Cheap builds make validation matter more, not less, because her time, attention, and momentum are still finite  ✅
- **C.** She should split the difference, building a stripped down version first and interviewing only if the launch stalls
- **D.** Validation is mainly for expensive builds; a weekend project can safely skip interviews and iterate after launching

> **Answer:** B

### Q16. A builder is drafting her interview script before talking to insurance brokers. Which question set matches the module's problem interview script?

- **A.** Would this tool help your agency, which features matter most to your team, and how much would you pay for it monthly
- **B.** How do you feel about your current software, what would your dream solution look like, and can I show you a demo
- **C.** Do you experience this problem, would you switch from your current tool, and would you join a beta list if I built it
- **D.** Tell me about the last time you dealt with this, what did you do, what have you tried, and what did that cost you  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q17. Halfway through an interview, a builder reveals her app idea, and the second half of the conversation turns glowing and agreeable. How should she treat that second half in her log?

- **A.** As stronger data, because the interviewee could finally react to something concrete instead of abstract questioning
- **B.** As equally valid data, if she marks in the log which answers came before the reveal and which came after it
- **C.** As contaminated data, because after the reveal the interviewee was responding to her instead of their own reality  ✅
- **D.** As bonus solution validation, letting her merge Module 2 and Module 3 work into one efficient single conversation

> **Answer:** C

### Q18. Interviewees confirm a real annoyance for a builder's idea, but it surfaces roughly once a year and they mostly shrug it off. What is the verdict?

- **A.** Demand is fine; annual problems can support a business if the tool is priced high enough to match the yearly stakes
- **B.** Demand is weak; a problem people hit rarely and shrug off fails the frequency and pain markers a business needs  ✅
- **C.** Demand is unclear; he should build a small version and measure whether usage clusters around that annual moment
- **D.** Demand is latent; infrequent problems turn frequent once a good tool makes people notice how often they occur

> **Answer:** B

### Q19. A builder validating a tool for Etsy sellers lists interview candidates: his sister who browses Etsy, two coworkers, and strangers from an Etsy seller forum. Whose answers count as validation?

- **A.** All of them, because any early conversation produces some useful signal as long as he anchors it in past behavior
- **B.** Only the strangers from the seller forum, because real validation requires strangers matching his customer profile  ✅
- **C.** The sister and coworkers first, since warm contacts let him practice the script before spending real prospects on it
- **D.** Whoever responds fastest, because interview volume matters more than sourcing at the earliest stage of validation

> **Answer:** B

### Q20. A builder wants a willingness to pay signal from a problem interview without ever pitching. Which question does the module endorse?

- **A.** If I built something that solved this completely, what would a fair monthly price feel like for your business today
- **B.** Would you commit to paying fifty dollars a month if the tool saved you at least five hours every single week
- **C.** How much do you think tools in this space usually charge, and does that range seem fair for the value they provide
- **D.** What did this problem cost you the last time it happened, and what have you already spent trying to deal with it  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q21. A builder wonders why the module is so strict about logging every interview when she remembers the conversations well. What purpose does the evidence log actually serve?

- **A.** It is mainly a motivational tool, giving her a visible record of effort that sustains momentum between the modules
- **B.** It exists mostly for early investors, who expect to review raw interview notes before committing any real capital
- **C.** It keeps her honest against memory bias and becomes the backbone of the Validated Idea Brief built in Module 7  ✅
- **D.** It trains her AI assistant, which needs structured interview data before it can help design the product well

> **Answer:** C

### Q22. A builder is proud of having discussed his idea in over a hundred casual chats with friends. How does the module weigh this against stranger interviews?

- **A.** Ten interviews with strangers sourced from where his market gathers beat a hundred chats with supportive friends  ✅
- **B.** A hundred conversations is a strong sample; he only needs to reword his questions to focus on specific past behavior
- **C.** Friend chats and stranger interviews are both useful, as long as the stranger data is weighted more heavily in his log
- **D.** He should now aim for a hundred stranger interviews, since validation confidence scales directly with interview volume

> **Answer:** A

### Q23. A builder has drafted her target customer profile, interview questions, and early evidence log. Before running more interviews, how does the module tell her to use her AI?

- **A.** As a stand in interviewee, roleplaying target customers so she can rehearse the script without wasting real prospects
- **B.** As a builder, generating a quick clickable prototype so interviewees can react to something concrete in the sessions
- **C.** As a blunt validation coach that flags pitching, leading, and hypothetical questions, and audits her evidence  ✅
- **D.** As a recruiter, drafting outreach messages and finding the forums and groups where her target market tends to gather

> **Answer:** C

### Q24. An interviewee hits a reporting problem weekly, maintains a messy spreadsheet workaround, and says her boss already budgets for tools in this area. Classify this problem.

- **A.** Nice-to-have, because a functioning spreadsheet means the problem is solved well enough to remove the pain
- **B.** Need-to-have, because urgency, available budget, and an existing workaround are exactly the markers of real demand  ✅
- **C.** Nice-to-have, because a serious buyer would have purchased proper software instead of tolerating a messy spreadsheet
- **D.** Inconclusive, because one interviewee cannot establish demand and classification must wait until interviews finish

> **Answer:** B

### Q25. Which statement best captures the governing principle of Module 2: Problem Validation?

- **A.** Build fast and launch early, because real market feedback on a live product beats any number of structured interviews
- **B.** Refine the pitch until your idea excites everyone who hears it, then build with full confidence in the coming demand
- **C.** Prove a real problem exists through evidence of past behavior from target market strangers before you build anything  ✅
- **D.** Choose the largest market you can find, because even mild problems become strong businesses at sufficient scale

> **Answer:** C
