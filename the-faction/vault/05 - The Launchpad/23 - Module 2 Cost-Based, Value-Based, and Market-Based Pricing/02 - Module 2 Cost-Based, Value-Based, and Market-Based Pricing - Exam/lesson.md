# Module 2: Cost-Based, Value-Based, and Market-Based Pricing — Exam

> Exam for **Module 2: Cost-Based, Value-Based, and Market-Based Pricing** (The Launchpad) — 25 questions, pass mark 80%. Correct answers are marked ✅ (read from each question's own post record; no quiz attempt was started to get them).

### Q1. A builder totals their hours, multiplies by a rate, adds 30 percent, and quotes. What method is this, and what is its blind spot?

- **A.** Market-based: it tracks the going rate, but it assumes that the work truly is comparable elsewhere
- **B.** Value-based: it prices the outcome, but it depends on numbers the client must accept
- **C.** Hybrid pricing: it blends lenses, but it inherits the weaknesses of every component
- **D.** Cost-based: it works up from effort, so it ignores what the outcome is worth to the client  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q2. A builder claims their cost for a project is '25 hours, nothing else.' Which cost categories are missing from that math?

- **A.** Only taxes: hour counts already absorb tooling since subscriptions bill monthly anyway
- **B.** Only revisions: everything else scales with hours and is captured inside the rate
- **C.** Nothing, if the hourly rate was set high enough to swallow incidental overheads
- **D.** Tools, API spend, hosting, admin time, taxes, and unbillable selling and scoping time  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q3. A build will save a firm 40,000 a year. The builder starts from that number and prices downward from the outcome. Which method is in use?

- **A.** Cost-based, since the savings estimates ultimately trace back to the builder's own effort
- **B.** Value-based: the price is set as a fraction of what the solution is worth to the client  ✅
- **C.** Market-based, since savings figures are how comparable projects get benchmarked
- **D.** Anchor pricing, since the 40,000 functions as the opening number in the negotiation

> **Answer:** B

### Q4. Market-based pricing keeps quotes inside the going range. What assumption does it quietly make, and why does that matter for positioned builders?

- **A.** That rates are public, which matters because most real project quotes never get published
- **B.** That demand is stable, which matters because AI is shifting what buyers will pay
- **C.** That buyers compare quotes, which matters because many decide on referrals alone
- **D.** That your work is comparable, which is exactly what strong positioning fights against  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q5. Under cost-based logic, a vibecoder who delivers in four days instead of six weeks ends up quoting less. Why does this method betray them?

- **A.** It ties price to effort, so efficiency gets converted into a discount for the client  ✅
- **B.** It ignores tooling costs, which run far higher for AI-directed builds than manual ones
- **C.** It requires timesheets, which AI-compressed projects cannot produce credibly
- **D.** It assumes long timelines, so short projects break its margin arithmetic entirely

> **Answer:** A

### Q6. Same builder, same four-day delivery, but priced on the value of the outcome. What happens to the speed advantage now?

- **A.** It transfers to the client as a lower price, since outcomes arrive sooner than quoted
- **B.** It disappears from the equation, since value math never references time at all
- **C.** It becomes margin: the outcome and price hold, and the compressed time is yours  ✅
- **D.** It becomes risk: faster delivery invites scrutiny of whether the price was inflated

> **Answer:** C

### Q7. In the floor-frame-target discipline, what exactly is the floor?

- **A.** The lowest market quote found in research, marking where the segment bottoms out
- **B.** Loaded cost plus minimum acceptable margin; below it, the answer is to decline  ✅
- **C.** The client's stated budget, since no quote above it can survive procurement
- **D.** Half the target price, giving negotiations a defined range to move within

> **Answer:** B

### Q8. A builder computes all three lenses on a serious quote. Which assignment of roles is correct?

- **A.** Value is the floor, cost is the target, market is the tiebreak when they disagree
- **B.** Market is the floor, value is the frame, cost is the target the quote aims for
- **C.** Cost sets the floor, market supplies the context, value sets the target price  ✅
- **D.** Each lens produces a quote, and the median of the three becomes the final price

> **Answer:** C

### Q9. A builder consistently feels busy but ends each month barely ahead. Their quotes cover build hours at a decent rate. What is the likeliest structural cause?

- **A.** Hidden overhead never got priced in, so each project quietly runs below break-even  ✅
- **B.** Rate decay: hourly rates lose value over time unless raised on a fixed schedule
- **C.** Overdelivery: finishing early leaves paid hours unbilled under any hourly arrangement
- **D.** Seasonality: builder demand clusters, and slow months erase strong ones' gains

> **Answer:** A

### Q10. A client wants a tiny internal tool whose benefit is real but genuinely hard to quantify. Which pricing approach fits?

- **A.** Value-based with estimated benefits, since every project deserves outcome pricing
- **B.** Market-based against enterprise tooling, since internal tools compete with vendors
- **C.** Cost-plus with honestly loaded costs: fine for small work with unquantifiable value  ✅
- **D.** Free, positioned as a relationship investment that seeds larger future projects

> **Answer:** C

### Q11. A build will measurably recover six figures of annual waste for a logistics firm. Which method should set the target price?

- **A.** Cost-based, since large engagements need effort math that procurement can audit
- **B.** Market-based, since six-figure outcomes attract competitors whose rates matter most
- **C.** Hourly, since big projects change shape too often for any fixed outcome price
- **D.** Value-based: measurable, large value is exactly where outcome pricing belongs  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q12. Research shows comparable work selling at 5,000 to 7,000. A positioned builder was planning to quote 12,000. How should the market data be used?

- **A.** As a hard ceiling: quoting beyond the researched range prices the builder out of the market
- **B.** As context: the quote needs a differentiation story, not automatic obedience to the range  ✅
- **C.** As a correction: split the difference and quote 9,000 to stay defensibly close
- **D.** As noise: market research only matters for commodity work, so discard it here

> **Answer:** B

### Q13. A proposal claims the build 'delivers 50,000 in annual value' but the number came from nowhere the client recognizes. What is this, and what happens next?

- **A.** Standard practice: value estimates are always directional, and clients discount them
- **B.** Conservative framing: unrecognized numbers are safer than aggressive client math
- **C.** Value theater: pricing on invented numbers collapses when the client checks the math  ✅
- **D.** Anchor setting: the figure's job is psychological, so its source never gets audited

> **Answer:** C

### Q14. The client disputes the savings model behind a value-based quote. What does the discipline require before any price is discussed further?

- **A.** Rebuild the value math with the client's own numbers until both sides recognize it  ✅
- **B.** Shift to cost-based pricing, since contested value math cannot anchor a quote
- **C.** Hold the quote firm, since conceding on the model concedes on the price behind it
- **D.** Split the difference between both models, since perfect value math never exists

> **Answer:** A

### Q15. An automation priced at 9,500 closed against an agency quote of 15,000, with 31,000 a year of documented waste. Why did this number work?

- **A.** It sat inside market context and well under first-year value, so it read as the safe buy  ✅
- **B.** It undercut the agency quote by a third, and undercutting is what closes competitive deals
- **C.** It matched the client's unspoken budget, which research had surfaced beforehand
- **D.** It was low enough to skip procurement review, which shortened the sales cycle

> **Answer:** A

### Q16. After computing the floor, a builder finds the client's ceiling sits below it. What does the discipline say?

- **A.** Take it once for the relationship, flagging that future work returns to normal rates
- **B.** Meet the ceiling but bill the gap as deferred revenue against the next engagement
- **C.** Take it if the portfolio needs the case study, treating the loss as marketing spend
- **D.** Decline or rescope: no pricing method justifies working below the computed floor  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q17. A builder directs AI to compute their loaded cost. Which input list produces an honest answer?

- **A.** Hours, tool stack costs amortized, API estimates, hosting, and admin overhead share  ✅
- **B.** Hours and the model subscription, since those two dominate every builder's costs
- **C.** Last year's revenue divided by projects, since averages absorb every hidden cost
- **D.** Competitor prices minus target margin, reverse-engineering cost from the market

> **Answer:** A

### Q18. AI research returns market ranges for portal builds. What must happen before those ranges inform a quote?

- **A.** Nothing: aggregated AI research is already more reliable than anecdotal rates
- **B.** Averaging: collapse the ranges into one midpoint number the quote can reference
- **C.** Inflation adjustment: posted rates lag the market by a year and need correcting
- **D.** Checked against real data points: posted rates, builder numbers, client quotes  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q19. A builder prices projects monthly and rebuilds cost math from scratch each time. What is the better practice?

- **A.** Memorize a standard floor that applies to every project regardless of shape
- **B.** Have AI build a reusable calculator: inputs in, floor out, consistent every time  ✅
- **C.** Price annually: set rates each January and hold them fixed across the year's quotes
- **D.** Outsource pricing to a fractional CFO once revenue justifies the recurring expense

> **Answer:** B

### Q20. When quantifying a client's problem for a value model, which form should the output take?

- **A.** The builder's own currency and rate structure, keeping every model internally consistent
- **B.** The client's own numbers and terms, stated per month and per year, recognizably theirs  ✅
- **C.** Industry benchmark units, since neutral figures survive procurement scrutiny best
- **D.** A single conservative annual figure, since ranges invite negotiation over the middle

> **Answer:** B

### Q21. A builder asks AI to price a build three ways: cost-plus, value-based, and market-anchored. What makes this exercise complete?

- **A.** A recommendation with reasoning: three numbers without a decision is not pricing  ✅
- **B.** A weighted average of the three, since blending removes each method's known bias
- **C.** Client review of all three, since transparency about method builds lasting trust
- **D.** A sensitivity table showing how each price moves as the input assumptions shift

> **Answer:** A

### Q22. A four-day, AI-directed build closes at 9,500. A skeptic calls the effective rate 'unearned.' What is the grounded response?

- **A.** The rate is temporary: competition will compress it as more builders adopt AI
- **B.** The rate is luck: one strong close does not validate a repeatable pricing method
- **C.** The price bought the outcome: the client captured 21,000 of first-year value too  ✅
- **D.** The rate reflects risk: short engagements concentrate delivery risk per day worked

> **Answer:** C

### Q23. In a healthy value-based deal, what does the split of created value look like?

- **A.** The builder captures most of it, since the builder's method is what created the value
- **B.** An even split, negotiated openly, since fairness sustains long relationships
- **C.** The client keeps the larger share; the price takes a defensible fraction of value  ✅
- **D.** The split is irrelevant, since value math exists only to justify the anchor

> **Answer:** C

### Q24. A builder pays 180 a month for their tool stack and runs about four projects monthly. How does this cost enter a project's loaded cost honestly?

- **A.** Amortized: roughly 45 of subscription cost lands on each project's cost line  ✅
- **B.** Fully: each project should carry the whole 180, building in a safety cushion
- **C.** Not at all: subscriptions are business overhead, unrelated to project pricing
- **D.** By usage audit: track tool minutes per project and allocate to the exact split

> **Answer:** A

### Q25. Across all three methodologies, what is the governing rule this module leaves you with?

- **A.** Choose one method early and apply it everywhere, since consistency builds trust
- **B.** Anchor the price to the outcome's worth, with cost below and market as the sanity check  ✅
- **C.** Average the three methods together, since each corrects the biases of the other two lenses
- **D.** Let the client pick the method, since buyers accept prices they help construct

> **Answer:** B
