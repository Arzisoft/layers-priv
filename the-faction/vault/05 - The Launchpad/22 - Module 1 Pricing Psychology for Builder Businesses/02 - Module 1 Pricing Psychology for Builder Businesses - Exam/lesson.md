# Module 1: Pricing Psychology for Builder Businesses — Exam

> Exam for **Module 1: Pricing Psychology for Builder Businesses** (The Launchpad) — 25 questions, pass mark 80%. Correct answers are marked ✅ (read from each question's own post record; no quiz attempt was started to get them).

### Q1. A builder agonizes for days over a quote, but the client reads it in ten seconds and moves on to timeline questions. What explains the mismatch?

- **A.** Clients skim proposals, so pricing pages get less attention than builders expect them to
- **B.** The price is a personal value claim to the builder; to the client it is one input in a decision  ✅
- **C.** The builder priced too low to trigger scrutiny, so the number passed without discussion
- **D.** Experienced clients hide their reactions in negotiations, so calm reading reveals nothing useful

> **Answer:** B

### Q2. In a pricing call, the first number spoken was 2,000, floated by the client. Every counter after that felt expensive. What principle is at work?

- **A.** Anchoring: the first number becomes the reference point all later numbers are judged against  ✅
- **B.** Reciprocity: once the client offers a figure, courtesy pressures the builder to move toward it
- **C.** Loss aversion: counters read as taking something away, which buyers resist instinctively
- **D.** Scarcity: early numbers feel rare and precious, so later revisions struggle for attention

> **Answer:** A

### Q3. A builder preparing for a call asks who should say a number first. What is the disciplined answer?

- **A.** The builder, anchoring with a well-framed range before the client can float a figure  ✅
- **B.** The client, since their number reveals budget information the builder can exploit
- **C.** Neither: numbers should arrive only in writing, after the call, inside the formal proposal
- **D.** Whoever has more leverage, since anchors only hold when backed by alternatives

> **Answer:** A

### Q4. Two security audit quotes arrive: one at 800, one at 6,500. The buyer cannot evaluate audit quality directly. How does the 800 price likely read?

- **A.** As a bargain the buyer should grab quickly before the auditor corrects the mistake
- **B.** As a loss leader: the cheap audit is bait for an expensive remediation contract later
- **C.** As a worse audit: buyers use price as a quality proxy when they cannot judge the work  ✅
- **D.** As roughly equal: buyers average competing quotes when expertise is hard to verify

> **Answer:** C

### Q5. A builder can present 6,000 as a bare figure or as '6,000 to stop losing 2,000 a month to manual processing.' What changes with the second framing?

- **A.** The price reads as an investment against a named cost instead of a standalone expense  ✅
- **B.** The price becomes legally binding, since it now references a measurable business outcome
- **C.** The price gains urgency, since monthly losses imply a deadline the client must beat
- **D.** Nothing substantive: sophisticated buyers strip framing and compare bare numbers anyway

> **Answer:** A

### Q6. A builder finishes stating a price, then immediately adds three paragraphs of unprompted reasons why it is fair. What did the client just learn?

- **A.** The builder is thorough, which supports the premium the price implies in this market
- **B.** The scope is well-considered, since detailed justifications reflect careful planning
- **C.** The market rate, since justification paragraphs typically reference competitor pricing
- **D.** The price is soft: nervous justification signals the number will move under pressure  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q7. After a quote, the prospect goes quiet for eight seconds. What is the disciplined read of that pause?

- **A.** Rejection forming: silence this long means the number has already lost the room
- **B.** A negotiation tactic: buyers manufacture pauses to invite panic concessions
- **C.** Confusion: the quote likely needs restating in simpler terms before it can land
- **D.** Thinking: silence after a price is processing, and filling it concedes ground  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q8. A client says the 9,000 proposal exceeds their budget, and the builder wants to keep the deal without gutting the value story. What is the right move?

- **A.** Cut the price to meet the budget while keeping scope intact, preserving goodwill
- **B.** Hold both price and scope firm, since moving either signals the quote was inflated
- **C.** Reduce scope to meet the budget, keeping the price-to-value relationship intact  ✅
- **D.** Offer staged payments at the same total, spreading the cost across milestones

> **Answer:** C

### Q9. Ten minutes before a pricing call, what preparation does the sequence in this course require?

- **A.** Rehearsing responses to the five most common objections in order of likelihood
- **B.** Deciding the number and the walk-away point, so live anchors cannot set them for you  ✅
- **C.** Researching the client's funding history to estimate the ceiling of their budget
- **D.** Drafting the follow-up email ahead of time, so the quote arrives in writing within the hour

> **Answer:** B

### Q10. A builder rejects their own 10,000 quote as 'too expensive' before any client sees it. The client loses 2,000 monthly to the problem. What mistake is forming?

- **A.** Overpricing: a number that startles its own author will certainly startle the buyer
- **B.** Scope inflation: discomfort with the number usually means the project grew too large
- **C.** Pricing from their own wallet: judging by personal budget instead of the client's problem  ✅
- **D.** Anchoring backwards: rejecting the number internally makes the builder concede faster externally

> **Answer:** C

### Q11. Which behavior teaches a client that a stated price is negotiable, before they ever object?

- **A.** Following the quote with unrequested justification, explaining the number nervously  ✅
- **B.** Quoting a round number, since round figures read as estimates rather than calculations
- **C.** Quoting in a range, since ranges reveal the floor the builder is prepared to accept
- **D.** Sending the quote by email, since written prices invite considered counteroffers

> **Answer:** A

### Q12. A builder prices every project cheap to win volume. Beyond thin margins, what does this strategy attract and set?

- **A.** Larger clients and higher expectations, since volume signals production capacity
- **B.** Referral momentum and better clients, since low prices maximize word of mouth
- **C.** Faster payment and lower risk, since small invoices clear without procurement
- **D.** Price-shoppers, crowded-out better clients, and a low anchor to fight later on  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q13. A prospect keeps asking about guarantees, references, and what happens if the build fails. The builder responds with a discount. What was misread?

- **A.** The buyer's authority: guarantee questions signal a hidden decision-maker upstream
- **B.** The timeline: risk questions usually mean the project deadline moved up internally
- **C.** The scope: failure questions suggest the buyer expects more than the proposal covers
- **D.** The buyer's sensitivity: this is risk-sensitivity, and it wanted proof, not cheapness  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q14. A prospect hesitates at 3,500 and the builder instantly drops to 2,800 unasked. The deal closes, then scope creep eats three weekends. Diagnose the pricing failure.

- **A.** The initial quote was too high for the segment, and the market corrected it properly
- **B.** The drop was too small: a decisive cut to 2,500 would have prevented the resentment
- **C.** A reactive discount: silence got read as rejection, and the price moved unprompted  ✅
- **D.** The failure was scoping, not pricing: creep would have happened at any price point

> **Answer:** C

### Q15. Before a call, a builder gives AI a few inputs: admin hours weekly, hourly cost, error rates. What is being built, and why?

- **A.** A competitor benchmark, so the quote lands inside the market's established range
- **B.** A cost-of-problem model, so the price can be framed against a named monthly loss  ✅
- **C.** A project estimate, so scope and hours justify the number if the client challenges
- **D.** A discount schedule, so concessions follow a plan instead of panic in the moment

> **Answer:** B

### Q16. A builder wants to stop flinching when quoting. Which use of AI trains this directly?

- **A.** Generating ten alternative prices, so the builder sees their number is defensible
- **B.** Drafting proposal language, so the written quote carries the emotional weight
- **C.** Mock negotiation with AI as a skeptical prospect who pushes back in three ways  ✅
- **D.** Sentiment analysis of past client emails, revealing which quotes caused friction

> **Answer:** C

### Q17. A builder keeps a simple table: every quote, the client's reaction, the outcome. Months later, what does this asset provide?

- **A.** Proof of consistency for clients who audit whether all buyers pay the same rates
- **B.** A compliance record, useful if a dispute over quoted terms ever reaches lawyers
- **C.** Material for testimonials, since the outcomes column doubles as a results archive
- **D.** Patterns from their own market: personal pricing data that beats generic advice  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q18. A community member asks whether this course tells them what to set aside for taxes and which entity to form. What is the correct answer?

- **A.** Yes: the later modules include tax percentages and entity comparisons by state
- **B.** Yes for taxes, no for entities, since entity choice depends on legal jurisdiction
- **C.** No: this is education, not financial advice; take those questions to an accountant  ✅
- **D.** No, but the community forum maintains an unofficial thread with those numbers

> **Answer:** C

### Q19. A builder accepts a project at a price they already resent. What should they expect from this engagement?

- **A.** The resentment will show in the work and the relationship; raise it or decline  ✅
- **B.** The resentment fades once payment clears, since money resolves pricing emotion
- **C.** The project will run fine if scope is documented, since contracts contain feeling
- **D.** The client will sense a bargain and refer others, compounding the low price's reach

> **Answer:** A

### Q20. A prospect's hesitation traces to doubt the build will work, not to the number. The builder is about to offer 15 percent off. Why is that the wrong tool?

- **A.** Discounts offered on first deals become permanent expectations across every later renewal
- **B.** Trust objections need proof: case studies and guarantees, not a cheaper version of the doubt  ✅
- **C.** Fifteen percent is too shallow to move a hesitant buyer; meaningful cuts start at thirty
- **D.** Discounting mid-conversation violates the anchor and resets negotiation from zero

> **Answer:** B

### Q21. After opening with the client's 1,900 monthly cost and anchoring at 9,000, the builder hears timeline questions instead of price questions. What happened?

- **A.** The client missed the price entirely, and the invoice will reopen the negotiation
- **B.** The anchor was too low: instant acceptance means money was left on the table
- **C.** The client is deferring conflict, and price objections will surface at contract time
- **D.** The frame held: against the named loss, 9,000 registered as a sound investment  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q22. A prospect asks 'what would something like this cost?' in the first five minutes, before any discovery. What is the disciplined response?

- **A.** Quote immediately but high, protecting margin against the unknowns still hiding
- **B.** Hold the number until scope is understood; quoting before scoping guesses twice  ✅
- **C.** Give last project's price as a reference, since real numbers build early trust
- **D.** Decline to discuss money on the first call as a standing policy, whatever is asked

> **Answer:** B

### Q23. A builder notices their new, higher price scares them a little each time they say it. How should they interpret that feeling?

- **A.** As normal at the right price: fear alone is not evidence the number should drop  ✅
- **B.** As a calibration error: correct prices feel neutral to the person quoting them
- **C.** As a ceiling signal: fear marks the exact top of what the market will accept
- **D.** As inexperience: after enough reps, any price should produce zero physical response

> **Answer:** A

### Q24. A client weighs an 8,000 automation build against hiring. Which comparison completes the framing honestly?

- **A.** Against the loaded cost of an in-house hire doing the same work over the same period  ✅
- **B.** Against the cheapest freelancer listing, showing that the builder sits mid-market safely
- **C.** Against the client's annual revenue, showing the build is a rounding error at scale
- **D.** Against the model API costs alone, showing how little of the price is raw compute

> **Answer:** A

### Q25. Beneath anchors, frames, and quoting mechanics, what governing truth does this entire foundation rest on?

- **A.** Every price is provisional: numbers exist to be negotiated toward a fair midpoint
- **B.** The client judges your price against their problem; only you judge it against your nerve  ✅
- **C.** Pricing is arithmetic: costs plus margin, stated clearly, closes at a natural rate
- **D.** The market sets the number: individual framing cannot move a rate the segment fixed

> **Answer:** B
