# Module 7: Raising Prices and Evolving Your Rate Card — Exam

> Exam for **Module 7: Raising Prices and Evolving Your Rate Card** (The Launchpad) — 25 questions, pass mark 80%. Correct answers are marked ✅ (read from each question's own post record; no quiz attempt was started to get them).

### Q1. A builder is booked past comfort, quotes get accepted without hesitation, and referrals arrive pre-sold. What do these conditions mean together?

- **A.** The niche is saturating: acceptance this easy usually signals commoditization ahead
- **B.** The market is already paying the next price; the rate card has not caught up  ✅
- **C.** The pipeline is lucky: two good quarters is variance, not an instruction
- **D.** Marketing is overperforming delivery, and expectations need managing down

> **Answer:** B

### Q2. What does it mean to call price 'a valve on demand'?

- **A.** Low prices open the valve fully, and full pipelines are the entire goal of pricing
- **B.** Raising price trades volume you cannot serve for margin you can actually keep  ✅
- **C.** Demand is fixed; the valve only chooses which clients flow through first
- **D.** Prices should oscillate with demand weekly, like surge pricing for builders

> **Answer:** B

### Q3. A builder's skills, speed, and proof have compounded for 18 months while rates stayed flat. What are those rates now?

- **A.** A quiet discount: the price describes a builder who no longer exists  ✅
- **B.** A stable anchor: consistency is worth more than any incremental raise
- **C.** Market rate: prices that close deals are by definition correctly set
- **D.** A safety margin: underpricing protects the pipeline in a downturn

> **Answer:** A

### Q4. Why do new prospects get the new rate before existing clients do?

- **A.** New prospects pay more readily because switching costs trap them early
- **B.** Existing clients legally require notice periods that new prospects do not
- **C.** New business absorbs mistakes: if the rate fails, no relationships are harmed
- **D.** They carry no anchor to the old number, making them the lowest-risk rollout  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q5. What do existing clients receive when the rate finally moves?

- **A.** A choice between the old rate with less service or the new rate unchanged
- **B.** A survey first, so the increase can be shaped around their own stated budgets
- **C.** The news at the next invoice, where numbers speak plainly for themselves
- **D.** Honest notice and runway: the new rate, its effective date, and appreciation  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q6. How should the increase conversation be delivered so it reads as maintenance rather than negotiation?

- **A.** With market data attached: comparable rates that prove the increase is modest
- **B.** As a question: asking clients whether the timing works keeps trust intact
- **C.** Plainly: the new rate and date, no apology spiral, no justification wall  ✅
- **D.** Through a proxy: written notice from an assistant depersonalizes the change

> **Answer:** C

### Q7. A rate increase email opens with three paragraphs of guilt and reasons. What does that framing invite?

- **A.** Negotiation on all of it: apology signals the increase itself is up for debate  ✅
- **B.** Sympathy: clients who understand the reasons accept increases more readily
- **C.** Churn: honest vulnerability reads as instability and clients quietly leave
- **D.** Silence: over-explained increases tend to get ignored rather than answered directly

> **Answer:** A

### Q8. A builder wants to keep one loyal client on legacy pricing. What gives grandfathering a defensible shape?

- **A.** Secrecy: legacy rates work only while other clients cannot discover them
- **B.** A defined period, a stated gap to the new rate, and an explicit endpoint  ✅
- **C.** Reciprocity: the client agrees to referrals in exchange for the old rate
- **D.** Simplicity: one flat legacy rate for all old clients, forever, no tiers

> **Answer:** B

### Q9. Years of open-ended grandfathering have left a builder with seven clients on five different old rates. What has this become?

- **A.** A shadow rate card that grows more expensive to honor with every passing year  ✅
- **B.** A loyalty program: tenure-based pricing that rewards the oldest relationships
- **C.** A healthy portfolio: diverse rates hedge the business against market swings
- **D.** A rounding error: legacy clients rarely account for meaningful revenue share

> **Answer:** A

### Q10. A builder's proof now justifies rates 60 percent above their card. What must happen for that jump to be credible?

- **A.** Repositioning: change what the number attaches to, then price the new position  ✅
- **B.** Gradualism: five small raises spaced over two years reach the same place safely
- **C.** An announcement: publish the case studies alongside the new number and hold firm
- **D.** A rebrand: new name and site, so the market cannot compare against old rates

> **Answer:** A

### Q11. The same 60 percent raise lands differently on an unchanged offer versus a repositioned one. What is the difference?

- **A.** Unchanged reads as confidence; repositioned reads as covering for real weakness
- **B.** No difference: acceptance depends on the client's budget, not the framing
- **C.** Unchanged wins: consistency of offer makes any price movement more legible
- **D.** Unchanged reads as arbitrary; repositioned reads as the rate for a new thing  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q12. What belongs on a rate card built as a living document?

- **A.** Rates by engagement type, tiers, minimums, rush terms, and a change log  ✅
- **B.** One number: a single hourly rate, since simplicity is what cards are for
- **C.** Client names beside their rates, so every negotiation starts from history
- **D.** Only public packages, since internal rates invite drift the moment they exist

> **Answer:** A

### Q13. How often should most builders review the rate card, and why that cadence?

- **A.** Monthly: pricing is dynamic and slow reviews leave money uncollected
- **B.** Only at signal moments: scheduled reviews produce changes without cause
- **C.** Twice a year: frequent enough to catch drift before it compounds quietly  ✅
- **D.** Annually with taxes: pricing and accounting belong in the same review

> **Answer:** C

### Q14. A builder says they will raise rates 'when clients indicate it is acceptable.' What is wrong with this plan?

- **A.** Clients indicate readiness constantly; the builder is misreading the signals
- **B.** It is fine: consent-based pricing is the most durable form of raising rates
- **C.** It underuses AI: sentiment analysis could detect acceptability directly
- **D.** No client will ever suggest it; the signal comes from demand, not kindness  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q15. New-business rates went up this month. What should the builder do before touching existing client rates?

- **A.** Nothing: both rollouts should land together so the card stays coherent
- **B.** Watch acceptance at the new rate for a full cycle; let the market prove it  ✅
- **C.** Poll existing clients about tolerance, converting notice into a soft survey
- **D.** Raise them immediately: a delay creates two classes of client permanently

> **Answer:** B

### Q16. How much runway should retainer clients get when receiving an increase?

- **A.** One billing cycle: thirty days matches how retainers are already invoiced
- **B.** Six months minimum, since retainers budget across half-year horizons
- **C.** Sixty to ninety days, generous enough to plan around without going stale  ✅
- **D.** Effective immediately, with the first month at the old rate as a courtesy

> **Answer:** C

### Q17. Which analysis should a builder direct AI to run before deciding a raise is due?

- **A.** Quote acceptance rate, time-to-yes, and capacity over the last two quarters  ✅
- **B.** Competitor price scraping across the niche's top twenty published rate cards
- **C.** A churn model scoring which clients would survive a 40 percent raise
- **D.** Inflation indexing, so the raise can be framed as cost-of-living adjustment

> **Answer:** A

### Q18. After a raise, one prospect declines the new rate. Capacity opens. What does the playbook expect to happen next?

- **A.** A quiet return to the old rate for the next prospect, protecting the pipeline
- **B.** The freed capacity absorbs a better-fit client at the new rate within weeks  ✅
- **C.** A losing streak: one balk usually begins a visible acceptance decline
- **D.** A grandfathering offer: decliners get legacy pricing to stop the bleeding

> **Answer:** B

### Q19. A loyal legacy client runs on thin margins and cannot absorb the new rate. What does a designed response look like?

- **A.** Exemption: some relationships transcend rate cards and should stay untouched
- **B.** Termination: clients who cannot afford the new rate no longer fit the business
- **C.** A defined bridge: twelve months at a stated gap to the card, then migration  ✅
- **D.** Effort reduction: same rate, quietly less service, until the margin balances

> **Answer:** C

### Q20. When setting the new number for a raise, what anchors it?

- **A.** A standard percentage: ten to fifteen percent yearly, whatever the context
- **B.** Current value delivered, repriced through the same lenses used for any quote  ✅
- **C.** The oldest client's tolerance, since the raise must survive its weakest link
- **D.** Round numbers: rates ending in zero signal confidence and simplify quoting

> **Answer:** B

### Q21. A rate changes on the card. Beyond the new number, what does the discipline record?

- **A.** The client reactions: who accepted, who pushed back, and who went quiet
- **B.** Nothing else: extra records invite second-guessing of settled decisions
- **C.** A market snapshot: competitor rates on the day the change was made
- **D.** The change itself in a log, and the next review date, before closing  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q22. The rate card changed Tuesday. The public pricing pages still show old numbers Friday. What problem is reforming?

- **A.** Document drift: card and pages disagreeing recreates the two-sources problem  ✅
- **B.** A legal exposure: published prices are binding offers in most jurisdictions
- **C.** An SEO penalty: stale pricing pages lose ranking against fresher competitors
- **D.** Nothing yet: pages lag cards harmlessly until a prospect actually books

> **Answer:** A

### Q23. A builder wants to know how the new revenue level changes their quarterly tax setaside. Where does that question go?

- **A.** To the pricing journal, which tracks every financial pattern in the business
- **B.** To AI, which can model setasides from the revenue data already on hand
- **C.** To their accountant: revenue effects are that conversation, not this course  ✅
- **D.** To the rate card changelog, where revenue changes are already being recorded

> **Answer:** C

### Q24. A well-run 35 percent raise settles in. What did the business gain, structurally?

- **A.** A newer client base: raises rotate portfolios toward better-funded buyers
- **B.** Pricing immunity: the next raise will be easier because this one succeeded
- **C.** Slack time: higher rates fund shorter weeks at identical revenue levels
- **D.** More revenue on identical delivery hours: margin created by price alone  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q25. What governing idea closes this course's final chapter on pricing?

- **A.** Prices are permanent: a number well-set at the start should rarely move
- **B.** Raises are gambles: each one risks the base that took years to assemble
- **C.** Prices grow with capability: raising them is routine maintenance, not risk  ✅
- **D.** Rates find their own level: markets correct mispricing without intervention

> **Answer:** C
