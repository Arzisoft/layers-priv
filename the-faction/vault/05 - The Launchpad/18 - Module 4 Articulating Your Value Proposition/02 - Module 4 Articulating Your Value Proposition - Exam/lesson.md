# Module 4: Articulating Your Value Proposition — Exam

> Exam for **Module 4: Articulating Your Value Proposition** (The Launchpad) — 25 questions, pass mark 80%. Correct answers are marked ✅ (read from each question's own post record; no quiz attempt was started to get them).

### Q1. A builder is checking a draft sentence against the one-sentence test. Which three elements must a stranger be able to find in it?

- **A.** What the builder does, for whom they do it, and why it matters to that audience  ✅
- **B.** The builder's name, their credentials, and the platforms their systems run on
- **C.** The price range, the timeline, and the guarantee that removes the buyer's risk
- **D.** The niche's biggest problem, the competitors who fail at it, and the proof of success

> **Answer:** A

### Q2. A builder proudly shares their line: I leverage AI to build custom automation solutions. The module says it fails the test twice. Where are the two failures?

- **A.** Too long for a headline, and the word leverage reads as corporate jargon
- **B.** No whom and no why: nobody specific is named, and no outcome explains the point  ✅
- **C.** No credentials and no proof: the claim floats without evidence behind it
- **D.** No platform and no price: buyers cannot evaluate the offer's practicality

> **Answer:** B

### Q3. A mentor takes a draft sentence and appends one question to the end of it as a filter. What is the question, and what does surviving it prove?

- **A.** Says who? Surviving proves the claim carries authority a buyer can verify
- **B.** How much? Surviving proves the sentence anchors a price a buyer can accept
- **C.** So what? Surviving proves the sentence names an outcome the client already wants  ✅
- **D.** Compared to what? Surviving proves the sentence positions against alternatives

> **Answer:** C

### Q4. Two lines describe one service: I build AI chatbots, versus: your team stops answering the same forty questions and follow-up leads stop going cold. What separates them, per the module?

- **A.** Tone: the second is conversational while the first is appropriately formal
- **B.** Scope: the second promises two outcomes while the first wisely promises one
- **C.** Accuracy: the first describes the work honestly while the second oversells it
- **D.** Category: the first is a capability description, the second is a value proposition  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q5. A builder has a list of technical capabilities and needs to translate each one for buyers. Into which three currencies does the module say every capability maps?

- **A.** Time saved, money gained or protected, and risk reduced for the business  ✅
- **B.** Speed, scale, and security, the three axes every software buyer evaluates
- **C.** Novelty, complexity, and defensibility, the measures of technical worth
- **D.** Features, benefits, and advantages, the classic sales translation ladder

> **Answer:** A

### Q6. A builder keeps pitching automated data extraction and getting blank stares. Which rewrite does the module's method produce?

- **A.** Enterprise-grade data extraction, adding the qualifier buyers associate with quality
- **B.** Your team stops retyping invoices for six hours a week, naming the outcome in work terms  ✅
- **C.** OCR-driven document parsing with validation, adding precision for the technical evaluators
- **D.** Data extraction starting at $2,000, adding the price to filter unqualified leads

> **Answer:** B

### Q7. Builders say automation, integration, workflow. Clients say: I do invoices on Sundays, the phone never stops. What does the module conclude from this vocabulary gap?

- **A.** Clients need education: part of selling is teaching buyers the proper terms
- **B.** Both vocabularies work: what matters is confidence in delivery, not word choice
- **C.** Builders should mix both registers so technical and business readers each find footing
- **D.** The strongest propositions are written in the client's words, not the builder's  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q8. A builder sits down to harvest the exact language their niche uses to complain. Which set of sources does the module send them to?

- **A.** Industry whitepapers, analyst webinars, and vendor marketing pages for the niche
- **B.** Their own imagination, refined through several rounds of AI-assisted brainstorming
- **C.** Validation call notes, competitor reviews, forum threads, and niche job postings  ✅
- **D.** Bestselling business books, since authors have already distilled the market's pain

> **Answer:** C

### Q9. The harvested phrase is: chasing clients for documents. The builder's more professional instinct is: streamlining document collection workflows. Which goes in the sentence, and why?

- **A.** The harvested phrase, verbatim: the client's own words are what they recognize and repeat  ✅
- **B.** The professional phrasing: extra polish signals competence and helps justify premium pricing
- **C.** A blend of the two, so the sentence lands with both the operators and the executives
- **D.** Neither: pain language should be replaced with positive outcome language entirely

> **Answer:** A

### Q10. A builder writes: before working with me, your front desk spends three hours a day on intake calls; after, patients book themselves. What does this framing accomplish?

- **A.** It sets up a legal performance baseline, protecting the builder if the promised results ever slip
- **B.** It doubles the sentence length, giving the proposition much more room to persuade
- **C.** It makes the transformation concrete and easy to repeat to a partner who was not in the room  ✅
- **D.** It hides the price conversation, shifting the attention from cost to circumstance

> **Answer:** C

### Q11. One builder starts the value proposition from a blank page; another starts from a pile of harvested complaint phrases. Why does the module bet on the second?

- **A.** Blank pages invite perfectionism, and momentum matters more than material
- **B.** The material already contains the winning words: the audience wrote them itself  ✅
- **C.** Harvested phrases are legally safer, since invented claims can be challenged
- **D.** The second builder finishes faster, and speed of shipping decides the winner

> **Answer:** B

### Q12. The module's drafting process produces ten candidate sentences, not one. What happens to them next?

- **A.** The builder merges the best clauses of each into a single composite sentence
- **B.** AI scores them on clarity and the highest scorer ships to every surface
- **C.** They rotate weekly on the website until analytics crowns a champion
- **D.** The so-what test cuts the weak ones, and survivors get tested on real prospects  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q13. Weeks after a networking event, a contact introduces the builder using nearly their exact sentence, slightly garbled. What does the module read into this moment?

- **A.** The winning proposition announced itself: it traveled, which is the test that counts  ✅
- **B.** A risk surfaced: garbled versions dilute the message and need correcting fast
- **C.** A coincidence occurred: single repetitions prove memorability, not real effectiveness
- **D.** A ceiling appeared: word of mouth reaches less far than paid distribution

> **Answer:** A

### Q14. A builder opens every pitch with GPT-powered and AI-driven, and wonders why owners glaze over. What is the module's diagnosis?

- **A.** The terms are stale: newer model names would quickly restore the pitch's novelty
- **B.** The audience is wrong: technology-led pitches belong only in front of CTOs and engineers
- **C.** The pitch is fine: glazing over is a normal stage on the way to a considered yes
- **D.** The pitch leads with technology, but the client is buying an outcome, not a model  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q15. A draft sentence names dentists, gyms, and law firms, promising speed, savings, growth, and peace of mind. What does the module predict for it?

- **A.** Strong performance: multiple audiences multiply the surface area for a possible match
- **B.** It says nothing to anyone: crammed sentences dilute until no reader self-recognizes  ✅
- **C.** Legal exposure: promising four distinct outcomes creates four separate liabilities
- **D.** Solid A/B potential: the crowded draft splits neatly into four separately testable versions

> **Answer:** B

### Q16. Unable to find good complaint material, a builder invents dramatic pain language they are sure the niche must feel. What is the predictable failure?

- **A.** The invented pain is too dark, and negative framing suppresses response rates
- **B.** Competitors recognize the invention and publicly correct it, costing credibility
- **C.** Invented vocabulary does not match how the niche talks, so nobody self-recognizes  ✅
- **D.** The language works at first but collapses when prospects reach the sales call

> **Answer:** C

### Q17. The first draft sounds decent, so a builder ships it to every surface the same evening and moves on. Which step did they skip, and what does skipping cost?

- **A.** Testing which sentence prospects repeat back: they shipped a guess instead of a winner  ✅
- **B.** Legal review of the claims: unvetted public promises tend to invite disputes down the road
- **C.** A careful grammar pass: small errors on public surfaces quietly erode a buyer's trust
- **D.** Competitor comparison: the draft may accidentally echo a direct rival's positioning

> **Answer:** A

### Q18. A builder's website says Custom AI Solutions for Modern Businesses, and inquiries are rare. His validation notes show bookkeepers repeating one phrase: chasing clients for documents. What is the module's prescribed move?

- **A.** Add specificity to the current line: Custom AI Solutions for Small Business Finance
- **B.** Keep the line but buy traffic: the message needs volume before it needs revision
- **C.** Run a brand refresh: the words are fine but the site design fails to convert
- **D.** Rebuild the sentence around a harvested phrase, naming bookkeepers and their pain  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q19. The bookkeeper who heard the new sentence repeats it almost word for word to her professional association. What property made the sentence capable of that trip?

- **A.** Brevity: sentences under ten words survive retelling regardless of content
- **B.** Rhythm: the sentence's cadence made it pleasant enough to quote at length
- **C.** It was built from the industry's own words, so repeating it felt like talking shop  ✅
- **D.** Authority: the builder's confident delivery made the claim feel safe enough to repeat

> **Answer:** C

### Q20. Three real outcomes are on the table: saves two hours daily, reduces billing errors, looks more professional. The builder must lead with one. How does the module break the tie?

- **A.** Lead with professionalism, since image outranks efficiency for small owners
- **B.** Lead with whichever outcome the validation calls complained about the most  ✅
- **C.** Lead with the hours, since time savings always test best across niches
- **D.** Lead with all three in sequence, letting each reader pick their favorite

> **Answer:** B

### Q21. A technical buyer joins the call, and the builder wonders whether to abandon outcome language for architecture talk. What does the module permit?

- **A.** Adding the how for this audience, while the outcome still leads the sentence  ✅
- **B.** Swapping fully into technical language, since matching the room wins deals
- **C.** Splitting the pitch in two: outcomes for the owner, architecture for the engineer
- **D.** Declining the technical discussion until after the contract is signed

> **Answer:** A

### Q22. In this module's workflow, AI clusters complaints, extracts recurring phrases, and drafts ten candidate sentences. What remains the builder's job?

- **A.** Checking AI's clustering math, since grouping errors would quietly poison the drafts
- **B.** Picking the winner from real prospect reactions: judgment on evidence stays human  ✅
- **C.** Rewriting every AI draft by hand, since generated sentences cannot be shipped
- **D.** Timing the release, since only the builder knows the niche's buying season

> **Answer:** B

### Q23. A builder finalizes the winning sentence and updates only the website, leaving the old pitch on LinkedIn and in their spoken intro. What does the module say about this rollout?

- **A.** Sensible sequencing: surfaces should update one at a time to isolate the effects cleanly
- **B.** Fine indefinitely: the website is the place where buying decisions actually get made
- **C.** Cautious and smart: keeping the old variants live preserves useful message diversity
- **D.** Incomplete: the sentence goes everywhere at once so every surface runs the same test  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q24. Halfway through a builder's sentence, a prospect interrupts: wait, it chases the documents for me? What is this interruption, in the module's terms?

- **A.** A red flag: interruptions like this signal impatience with the pitch's pacing
- **B.** A stall tactic: repeating the claim back as a question buys the prospect thinking time
- **C.** Recognition: the prospect saw their own problem inside the sentence and grabbed it  ✅
- **D.** A misunderstanding: the prospect heard a feature the offer does not actually include

> **Answer:** C

### Q25. One principle governs this module, from the so-what test to the harvest. Which statement expresses it?

- **A.** Outcomes stated in the client's language sell; capabilities stated in builder language do not  ✅
- **B.** Precision sells: the more technically exact the sentence is, the more trust it earns from buyers
- **C.** Brevity sells: cut any sentence down until only the shortest possible claim remains standing
- **D.** Novelty sells: propositions win by sounding unlike anything the buyer has ever heard

> **Answer:** A
