# Module 4: Productized Service Pricing — Exam

> Exam for **Module 4: Productized Service Pricing** (The Launchpad) — 25 questions, pass mark 80%. Correct answers are marked ✅ (read from each question's own post record; no quiz attempt was started to get them).

### Q1. A builder notices the same automation appearing across many past projects with minor variations. What does productization do with that observation?

- **A.** It licenses the automation as software, replacing services revenue with product sales
- **B.** It archives the pattern as a template, cutting future custom quotes by a few hours
- **C.** It assigns the repeated work to subcontractors, freeing the builder for novel projects
- **D.** It standardizes the repeated core into a named offer: fixed scope, deliverables, price  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q2. The tenth delivery of a package costs the builder far less than the first, while the price stays constant. What is happening?

- **A.** Scope shrinkage: each delivery quietly trims deliverables the buyers never notice
- **B.** Price erosion in reverse: inflation raises the real value of the constant price
- **C.** Margin compounding: prompts, templates, and infrastructure get reused per sale  ✅
- **D.** Loss recovery: early deliveries ran below cost and later ones repay the deficit

> **Answer:** C

### Q3. A builder wonders which of their services is ready to become a package. What is the qualifying signal?

- **A.** Client requests: any service a prospect has asked to see priced deserves a package
- **B.** Profitability: the highest-margin service is the one to standardize first
- **C.** Repetition: a core delivered three or more times with only minor variation  ✅
- **D.** Simplicity: the easiest service to describe makes the strongest pricing page

> **Answer:** C

### Q4. What problem does a three-tier menu solve that a single fixed package cannot?

- **A.** Legal exposure: multiple tiers spread liability across separate scopes of work
- **B.** It serves small, standard, and premium buyers from one page, without custom quotes  ✅
- **C.** Refund risk: buyers who overreach can be moved down a tier instead of refunded
- **D.** Discovery: three options generate three times the analytics on buyer tier preference

> **Answer:** B

### Q5. In the good-better-best structure, which tier should be designed as the offer you actually want to sell?

- **A.** The middle: most buyers land there, so it carries the real offer by design  ✅
- **B.** The top: premium buyers fund the business, so the design energy goes there
- **C.** The bottom: entry buyers become upgrades, so the entry experience is the product
- **D.** All equally: favoring any tier distorts the menu and skews buyer trust downward

> **Answer:** A

### Q6. Beyond serving buyers who want everything handled, what job does the top tier do for the whole menu?

- **A.** It absorbs scope creep: overflow work from lower tiers lands there billably
- **B.** It anchors the range, making the middle tier read as the reasonable choice  ✅
- **C.** It funds the entry tier, whose margin alone cannot sustain the package line
- **D.** It filters buyers: anyone choosing it signals budget for future custom work

> **Answer:** B

### Q7. How should tiers differ from each other, according to the discipline?

- **A.** By scope and outcome: each tier is a complete, honest offer at its own level  ✅
- **B.** By speed: identical deliverables, with higher tiers jumping the delivery queue
- **C.** By effort: hours invested per tier justify the price steps between the tiers
- **D.** By access: the deliverables match, but higher tiers buy more meetings with you

> **Answer:** A

### Q8. An entry tier was stripped so hard it cannot actually solve the buyer's problem. What is this called, and what follows?

- **A.** A punishment tier: it poisons trust, and burned buyers do not return to upgrade  ✅
- **B.** A loss leader: it works as marketing even when the deliverable disappoints
- **C.** A qualifier: failing the cheap tier motivates the upgrade the menu intended
- **D.** Lean packaging: entry buyers expect less, so a thinner tier matches their expectations

> **Answer:** A

### Q9. A builder proposes a menu with six tiers to cover every buyer situation. What is the predictable result?

- **A.** Better conversion: more tiers means more precise matches for more budgets
- **B.** Choice overload: the page stalls buyers, and three tiers was already the ceiling  ✅
- **C.** Operational chaos only: buyers cope fine, but six delivery playbooks certainly will not
- **D.** Anchor dilution: six prices average out and the menu loses its premium signal

> **Answer:** B

### Q10. A genuinely novel project arrives that fits no package. What is the productized business supposed to do with it?

- **A.** Decline it: novel work breaks the repeatability the business model depends on
- **B.** Build a new tier for it immediately, so the menu can grow with each novel request
- **C.** Deliver the nearest package anyway, adapting the playbook during the build
- **D.** Quote it custom: the menu handles the repeatable, the quote path handles the new  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q11. A bespoke request lands from a prospect who has seen the builder's package menu. How does the menu affect the custom quote?

- **A.** It caps it: custom quotes above the top tier read as punishment for being unusual
- **B.** It is irrelevant: custom work is priced on its own value math, menu unseen
- **C.** It anchors it: the posted packages set the context bespoke pricing builds from  ✅
- **D.** It discounts it: menu buyers expect custom work at a loyalty rate below list

> **Answer:** C

### Q12. What belongs on a productized offer's pricing page?

- **A.** What you get, who it is for, price, timeline, process, and proof of past results  ✅
- **B.** The full contract terms, so procurement can approve it without a legal review pass
- **C.** The builder's full portfolio, so buyers can judge range beyond the package
- **D.** Cost breakdowns per deliverable, so buyers see exactly where the money goes

> **Answer:** A

### Q13. A prospect books a call already knowing the tiers, the price, and the timeline. What did the pricing page just do?

- **A.** It leaked negotiating room: informed prospects anchor below the posted rates
- **B.** It filtered out the enterprise buyers, who expect pricing conversations, not pages
- **C.** It replaced positioning: pages that state prices compete on price alone
- **D.** It answered the pricing conversation before it started; the call is about fit now  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q14. What is the first step in turning a year of custom work into a productized offer?

- **A.** Design the tiers, since the menu structure determines what history matters
- **B.** Mine the delivered projects for the repeated core, directing AI to find it  ✅
- **C.** Survey past clients about what they would have paid for faster delivery
- **D.** Build the pricing page, since public commitment forces the offer to finalize

> **Answer:** B

### Q15. A builder packages a service they have delivered once, estimating the scope for the other deliverables. What is the risk?

- **A.** Fixed pricing on unproven scope: the package is a gamble, not a product  ✅
- **B.** None, if the price includes a buffer: padding absorbs estimation error
- **C.** Slow sales: unproven packages convert at half the rate of proven ones
- **D.** Brand dilution: thin packages weaken the builder's premium positioning

> **Answer:** A

### Q16. Sales data shows buyers choosing only the top and bottom tiers, almost never the middle. What does this pattern indicate?

- **A.** Success: the extremes serve real segments and the middle can be retired
- **B.** Anchor failure: the top tier is priced far too close to the middle to do its job
- **C.** A healthy barbell: markets naturally split into premium and budget buyers
- **D.** The middle tier is mispriced or misdesigned; it exists to be the default choice  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q17. Intake questionnaires, kickoff checklists, and completion report templates ship with each package sale. What are these assets for?

- **A.** Running every delivery through the same playbook, so quality and cost stay level  ✅
- **B.** Marketing: process artifacts photograph well on pricing pages and proposals
- **C.** Compliance: documented steps protect the builder if a delivery gets disputed
- **D.** Training: the kit exists so future subcontractors can deliver without the builder

> **Answer:** A

### Q18. A builder tracks price, delivery hours, and tool costs for every package sale in a sheet. What is this discipline watching?

- **A.** Tax categories: per-sale records simplify quarterly filings for the accountant
- **B.** Client profitability: which buyers cost more to serve than their tier price covers
- **C.** Utilization: whether packaged work is crowding out higher-value custom projects
- **D.** Margin growth: whether delivery cost per sale actually falls as repetitions add up  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q19. What role does the entry tier play when it is designed correctly?

- **A.** Easy entry: a smaller but complete offer that genuinely works at its price  ✅
- **B.** A decoy: it exists to be declined, steering budgets toward the middle tier
- **C.** A trial: a refundable taste of the middle tier, credited against upgrades
- **D.** A filter: its friction screens out buyers too small to serve profitably

> **Answer:** A

### Q20. A menu prices its tiers by how many hours each takes the builder. Which familiar failure has been rebuilt inside the menu?

- **A.** The anchor trap: hour-based tiers cluster too close together to anchor anything
- **B.** Value theater: hour counts are invented numbers buyers cannot verify anyway
- **C.** The hourly trap: effort pricing caps margin and discounts the AI speed advantage  ✅
- **D.** Scope creep: hour-based tiers invite buyers to negotiate deliverables per hour

> **Answer:** C

### Q21. A prospect's project half-fits Intake Pro, and the builder squeezes it into the package to avoid writing a quote. What ships?

- **A.** A fine outcome: playbooks flex, and half-fit projects usually converge to the core
- **B.** A bad fit: forcing novel work into a package delivers the package, not a solution  ✅
- **C.** A hidden upsell: the misfit surfaces gaps that become a second engagement later
- **D.** A discount in disguise: the buyer gets custom attention at the package's price

> **Answer:** B

### Q22. What turns a pricing page from a brochure into a place where buyers can act?

- **A.** A countdown timer on each tier, converting casual menu interest into scheduled urgency
- **B.** A chat widget, so buyers can negotiate tier boundaries before committing
- **C.** Testimonials per tier, matching social proof to each buyer segment's doubts
- **D.** Payment links per tier, making each package directly transactable from the page  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q23. Before publishing a menu, a builder asks AI to stress-test the tiers. What is that review looking for?

- **A.** Grammar and layout issues that undermine the page's professional credibility
- **B.** Forced upgrades and cannibalization: where structure works against buyers  ✅
- **C.** Legal exposure: promises in tier copy that could be read as guarantees
- **D.** Price elasticity: how much each tier could rise before conversion suffers

> **Answer:** B

### Q24. After replacing custom quotes with a package menu, a builder's sales conversations on packaged deals drop from hours to minutes. What changed?

- **A.** Deal quality dropped: shorter conversations mean smaller, less serious buyers
- **B.** The builder got faster at pitching, a skill effect unrelated to the menu itself
- **C.** Buyers arrive pre-anchored: the page did the pricing conversation in advance  ✅
- **D.** Competition thinned: posted prices scared away rivals who quote custom only

> **Answer:** C

### Q25. What is the governing logic of productized pricing in one sentence?

- **A.** Custom work is dead: menus will replace quoting everywhere as AI compresses builds
- **B.** Packages exist to maximize volume: price low, sell often, and let scale pay you
- **C.** Sell the proven repeatable at a held price, and let falling delivery cost pay you  ✅
- **D.** Menus are marketing: real revenue still comes from the custom work behind them

> **Answer:** C
