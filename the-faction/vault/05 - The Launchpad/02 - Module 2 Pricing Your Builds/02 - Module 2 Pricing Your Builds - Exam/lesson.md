# Module 2: Pricing Your Builds — Exam

> Exam for **Module 2: Pricing Your Builds** (The Launchpad) — 25 questions, pass mark 80%. Correct answers are marked ✅ (read from each question's own post record; no quiz attempt was started to get them).

### Q1. A build will save a client roughly $150,000 per year in labor. What should anchor your price?

- **A.** The value delivered: price against the outcome's worth to them, not the hours it takes to produce  ✅
- **B.** Your hours alone: estimate the time honestly, multiply it by a fair rate, and present that number
- **C.** The AI subscription costs involved, plus a modest markup to cover your administrative overhead
- **D.** Whatever a traditional agency would charge, minus at least half so that the comparison always wins

> **Answer:** A

### Q2. Mid-project, the client asks for one more small feature for the third time. What is the professional response?

- **A.** Build it silently, since small accommodations are the cost of keeping any client relationship warm
- **B.** Welcome the request and route it through change control: new scope, priced and scheduled as an add  ✅
- **C.** Refuse all additions until the project ends, since mid-build changes are unprofessional to request
- **D.** Build it but slow down elsewhere to recover the time, keeping the trade fully invisible to the client

> **Answer:** B

### Q3. A reference model prices build modules at a fixed rate for a defined block of directed engineering. What makes this structure work for both sides?

- **A.** The client buys a predictable outcome at a known price, and the builder sells defined capacity, not open time  ✅
- **B.** The block is small enough that neither side ever needs a contract, which removes all of the legal friction
- **C.** The fixed rate guarantees the builder a profit on every module regardless of what was promised
- **D.** The structure hides the true hours from the client, which keeps the effective rate confidential

> **Answer:** A

### Q4. When does a monthly retainer beat project pricing for a builder?

- **A.** Always, since recurring revenue is strictly better than project revenue in every client situation
- **B.** Never, since retainers cap your income while projects let every new engagement be priced fresh
- **C.** Only with enterprise clients, since smaller companies are rarely able to commit to monthly billing
- **D.** When the client has ongoing needs: continuous improvements, maintenance, and new requests monthly  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q5. Why should you require a deposit before starting a first-time client's build?

- **A.** It confirms commitment, funds your early work, and filters out clients who never intended to pay  ✅
- **B.** It is required by law for software engagements above a minimum size in most business regions
- **C.** It lets you skip a written agreement, since payment itself functions as the binding contract
- **D.** It transfers all project risk over to the client, leaving you with no obligations if delivery slips

> **Answer:** A

### Q6. You priced far below market to win a deal, and the prospect became more hesitant, not less. Why?

- **A.** They were offended by the discount, since buyers expect every service to open at its full price
- **B.** Low prices trigger procurement reviews, which add hesitation to otherwise simple decisions
- **C.** Price signals quality; a number far below every alternative reads as risk instead of a bargain  ✅
- **D.** They wanted hourly billing, and low fixed prices make hourly conversion math too confusing

> **Answer:** C

### Q7. What is the advantage of presenting a proposal with two or three scoped options instead of one number?

- **A.** It triples the paperwork, which signals professional thoroughness better than a single flat option
- **B.** It shifts the decision from yes-or-no to which-one, and lets the client self-select their budget  ✅
- **C.** It confuses the client productively, since uncertain buyers default to the most expensive choice
- **D.** It removes the need for a discovery call, since options substitute for understanding the client

> **Answer:** B

### Q8. Your delivery quality and demand have both grown. How do rate increases work in practice?

- **A.** They don't; the rate you charge your first client becomes your permanent market rate forever
- **B.** Announce a retroactive increase, billing past clients the difference for previously finished work
- **C.** Raise rates only when a client suggests it, since self-initiated increases damage relationships
- **D.** Quote new work at the higher rate, and give existing clients notice before their pricing changes  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q9. A prospect you want asks for 20% off. If you choose to move, what is the disciplined way to do it?

- **A.** Give the discount unconditionally, since generosity early in a relationship compounds well later on
- **B.** Give it but inflate the next invoice quietly, recovering the margin without reopening the talk
- **C.** Trade it for something real: reduced scope, a case study, a referral, prepayment, or a longer term  ✅
- **D.** Refuse and end the conversation, since any discount request predicts a lifetime of demands

> **Answer:** C

### Q10. Your AI tooling costs a few hundred dollars monthly, and a client says your price should reflect those low costs. What is the flaw in that logic?

- **A.** Price reflects the value and accountability you deliver, not the input costs of your tools  ✅
- **B.** There is no flaw; professional pricing has always been tool costs plus a fixed percentage
- **C.** Tool costs are secret, so the client's estimate of your expenses is certainly too low anyway
- **D.** The flaw is timing; cost-based pricing works, but only after a first project establishes trust

> **Answer:** A

### Q11. How should your proposal handle the client's real alternatives, like hiring a developer or an agency?

- **A.** Never mention them, since naming alternatives plants options the client had not considered
- **B.** Disparage them directly, since undermining competitors is the entire purpose of a proposal document
- **C.** Match the agency price exactly, since identical pricing removes comparison from the decision
- **D.** Frame the comparison honestly: what each path costs in money and time, and where your offer wins  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q12. How should payments be structured on a multi-week fixed-price build?

- **A.** One hundred percent up front, since builders should never carry any receivable risk whatsoever
- **B.** Tied to milestones: a deposit, then payments as defined deliverables ship, and a final on handoff  ✅
- **C.** One hundred percent on completion, since clients should never pay for unfinished software
- **D.** Daily micro-invoices, since small constant payments keep both parties maximally attentive

> **Answer:** B

### Q13. Why price an entry offer like a platform audit low and fixed instead of custom-quoting it?

- **A.** Because audits have no delivery cost, so any revenue from them is pure margin at every price
- **B.** Because regulators cap what can be charged for assessment work in most service industries
- **C.** A low fixed price removes friction from the first yes, and the audit's output scopes the paid build  ✅
- **D.** Because clients compare audit pricing across vendors, and the lowest number always wins the deal there

> **Answer:** C

### Q14. Despite its weaknesses, when is hourly or day-rate billing actually the right choice?

- **A.** For genuinely undefined work: exploratory sessions, debugging unknowns, or advisory time with no set scope  ✅
- **B.** For every first engagement, since hourly is the only structure new clients will ever accept
- **C.** For the largest projects only, since big builds are too complex to scope into fixed pricing
- **D.** Whenever the client requests it, since payment structure is always the client's decision to make alone

> **Answer:** A

### Q15. A prospect demands a price in the first five minutes, before you understand the problem. What do you do?

- **A.** Give your standard number immediately, since hesitation on pricing reads as inexperience
- **B.** Quote high at random, since an inflated anchor protects you from whatever the scope becomes
- **C.** Refuse to ever discuss price until a signed agreement exists, since early numbers bind you
- **D.** Share your typical range or starting point, and explain how discovery will make it exact  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q16. Beyond your time, what must a sustainable build price account for?

- **A.** Nothing else; a solo builder's time is the only real cost, so revenue and profit are identical
- **B.** Tools, taxes, unbillable time like sales and admin, and margin to reinvest in the business  ✅
- **C.** Only taxes, since tool subscriptions are too small to meaningfully affect what you charge
- **D.** Office space above all, since a commercial address is the largest expense for any builder

> **Answer:** B

### Q17. In a proposal with several options, why present the most complete option first?

- **A.** It anchors the conversation at full value, making the middle option feel reasonable, not large  ✅
- **B.** Reading order is legally regulated, and the complete options are required to appear before others
- **C.** It hides the cheapest option, which clients will not notice once a large number appears first
- **D.** It guarantees the client chooses it, since first-position options win in almost every proposal

> **Answer:** A

### Q18. A prospect says that's too expensive. What is that objection most often actually telling you?

- **A.** Your price is objectively wrong, and the correct response is an immediate matching discount
- **B.** The prospect is bluffing, and holding silent eye contact will cause them to accept the full price
- **C.** They don't yet see value equal to the number, so revisit the outcome before touching the price  ✅
- **D.** The deal is dead, since price objections at any stage predict failed negotiations reliably

> **Answer:** C

### Q19. A prospect asks for a detailed build plan, architecture, and timeline before paying anything. Where is the line?

- **A.** There is no line; complete unpaid planning is the standard cost of competing for any project today
- **B.** Provide everything but watermark the documents, since marking them prevents any misuse
- **C.** Refuse to describe your approach at all, since every unpaid word is exploitable consulting
- **D.** A high-level approach is free; detailed plans are paid work, packaged as the audit or scoping phase  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q20. Why do experienced builders push part of their revenue toward retainers and recurring services?

- **A.** Recurring revenue is taxed at a lower rate than project revenue in most business jurisdictions
- **B.** It smooths the feast-and-famine cycle of project work and compounds the value of each client won  ✅
- **C.** Retainers require no delivery work, functioning as passive income once the agreement is signed
- **D.** Project pricing becomes illegal past a certain business size, forcing the shift to subscriptions

> **Answer:** B

### Q21. What is a walk-away number, and why should you set it before any negotiation?

- **A.** The price that guarantees you win the deal, calculated from the client's published budget figures alone
- **B.** The maximum a client can pay, which you should always discover by asking their revenue directly
- **C.** The floor below which the deal stops being worth doing, decided calmly in advance, not under pressure  ✅
- **D.** A negotiation trick where you threaten to leave twice before accepting whatever remains offered

> **Answer:** C

### Q22. Which principle should anchor how you price AI-directed builds overall?

- **A.** Charge for outcomes and accountability, put terms in writing, and let speed be your margin, not your discount  ✅
- **B.** Charge the minimum survivable rate, since underpricing is the only truly reliable acquisition strategy
- **C.** Copy a competitor's public rate card exactly, since matching the market removes all pricing risk
- **D.** Keep pricing improvised per deal, since consistency limits what each negotiation can extract

> **Answer:** A

### Q23. What is modular pricing for AI-directed builds?

- **A.** Charging separately for every file the AI produces, so invoices scale with the total size of the build
- **B.** Adjusting the price weekly based on demand, the way airlines and rideshare platforms set fares
- **C.** Letting the client name their own price for each part of the build after seeing it demonstrated
- **D.** Selling fixed blocks of defined scope at a set price, so clients buy predictable units of delivery  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q24. Why does pure hourly billing undervalue AI-directed engineering specifically?

- **A.** Hourly billing is illegal for software services in a growing number of business jurisdictions
- **B.** Your speed is the product; when AI direction finishes in hours what took weeks, hourly punishes you  ✅
- **C.** Clients categorically refuse hourly arrangements, so proposing one ends most of these deals immediately
- **D.** Hourly rates cannot be entered into modern invoicing platforms, which are built for flat fees

> **Answer:** B

### Q25. What protects you most when quoting a fixed price for a defined build?

- **A.** A verbal understanding with the client, since flexibility matters more than early documentation
- **B.** Quoting triple your estimate, so any conceivable surprise is absorbed inside the initial margin
- **C.** A written scope that states what is included and, just as explicitly, what is not included  ✅
- **D.** Weekly renegotiation checkpoints where the price can be adjusted as the true effort emerges

> **Answer:** C
