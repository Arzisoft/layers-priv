# Module 5: Writing Pricing Into Proposals and SOWs — Exam

> Exam for **Module 5: Writing Pricing Into Proposals and SOWs** (The Launchpad) — 25 questions, pass mark 80%. Correct answers are marked ✅ (read from each question's own post record; no quiz attempt was started to get them).

### Q1. In what order should a proposal unfold so the price reads as a conclusion?

- **A.** Investment first, then approach and proof, so serious buyers self-select early
- **B.** Approach, investment, problem, outcome, mirroring how the builder scoped the work
- **C.** Problem, outcome, approach, proof, then investment: the case before the number  ✅
- **D.** Proof first, then investment, then problem, leading with credibility over context

> **Answer:** C

### Q2. A proposal opens with the price on page one. What happens in the client's reading experience?

- **A.** The number gets judged before any context exists, and the document reads as a quote  ✅
- **B.** The client appreciates the transparency and reads the rest of it with far more trust
- **C.** The price anchors high, making all of the following pages feel like added value
- **D.** Nothing changes: decision-makers skip to the price no matter where it sits

> **Answer:** A

### Q3. A builder asks why pricing appears in both the proposal and the SOW. What is the division of labor?

- **A.** Redundancy: two documents stating the price makes disputes twice as unlikely
- **B.** Sequencing: the proposal holds the draft price while the SOW holds the final version
- **C.** The proposal frames the investment persuasively; the SOW states the precise terms  ✅
- **D.** Audience: the proposal price is for the champion, the SOW price for procurement

> **Answer:** C

### Q4. Why does the discipline cap pricing options at three?

- **A.** Three matches the way procurement templates are built, easing enterprise approval
- **B.** Three shapes a choice between versions of yes; more reopens the whole analysis  ✅
- **C.** Three is a printing convention: more options push the section past one page
- **D.** Three protects margin: beyond three, buyers average the prices and anchor low

> **Answer:** B

### Q5. How should the Investment section present a 12,000 price?

- **A.** As twelve line items summing to 12,000, so the client sees exactly what builds cost
- **B.** As a range around 12,000, leaving room for negotiation the client will expect
- **C.** One outcome sentence and the clean number, with the detail kept beneath or beyond  ✅
- **D.** As a monthly equivalent, since smaller numbers read easier than project totals

> **Answer:** C

### Q6. A proposal prices fifteen components separately. The client returns with eight of them circled. What did the format cause?

- **A.** Faster approval: itemization let the client skip an internal budgeting round
- **B.** An itemized negotiation: the wall of parts invited building a cheaper project  ✅
- **C.** Scope clarity: the circled items reveal what the client actually values the most
- **D.** A stronger anchor: fifteen numbers stack into a total that reads as thorough

> **Answer:** B

### Q7. A builder wins a 12,000 project but collects the money 90 days after delivery, with no deposit. What is this outcome, honestly named?

- **A.** A discount in disguise: weak terms quietly reduced what the strong price was worth  ✅
- **B.** A financing win: the builder effectively extended credit and earned client loyalty
- **C.** Normal enterprise rhythm: 90-day collection is the cost of serious clients
- **D.** A collections failure unrelated to pricing, best fixed with an invoicing tool

> **Answer:** A

### Q8. Besides funding the start of work, what does requiring a deposit do at signing time?

- **A.** It locks the scope: deposits legally freeze the SOW as written at payment
- **B.** It accelerates delivery: funded projects jump the queue in the builder's schedule
- **C.** It sets the anchor for the final invoice, which mirrors the deposit's size
- **D.** It filters unserious buyers: those unwilling to commit money reveal it early  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q9. What should trigger the final payment in a well-written SOW?

- **A.** The client's confirmation that they are satisfied with the overall result
- **B.** The calendar: a fixed date protects the builder from acceptance delays
- **C.** First production use, since real usage is the honest test of delivery
- **D.** Defined delivery criteria being met, stated in the SOW before work began  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q10. The proposal says 14,000; the attached SOW still says 12,500 from an earlier draft. What happens at invoice time?

- **A.** The proposal wins: sales documents take precedence over technical attachments
- **B.** The average wins: split-the-difference is the standard cure for any document drift
- **C.** A dispute the builder loses: the client reasonably pays the lower written number  ✅
- **D.** Nothing: totals are reconciled at signature, so drift is caught automatically

> **Answer:** C

### Q11. What single habit prevents proposal-SOW drift from reaching clients?

- **A.** A cross-check pass comparing every number in both documents before anything sends  ✅
- **B.** Sending the SOW a full week after the proposal, once its numbers have settled down
- **C.** Keeping prices out of the SOW entirely, so only one document carries numbers
- **D.** Round numbers only, since drift hides more easily inside the precise figures

> **Answer:** A

### Q12. Payment terms first appear on the builder's invoice, after the contract was signed. How do those terms land?

- **A.** As imposed: terms introduced after signing were never part of the agreed deal  ✅
- **B.** As standard: invoices are where payment terms conventionally live anyway
- **C.** As negotiable: post-signature terms open a second, smaller negotiation round
- **D.** As strict: late terms carry far more force when they arrive with the actual bill

> **Answer:** A

### Q13. Three options appear in a proposal. What does each option need for the section to work?

- **A.** A separate SOW per option, so the client can sign whichever version directly
- **B.** An expiry date per option, so the menu creates urgency alongside the choice
- **C.** A testimonial per option, matching proof to each level of the investment
- **D.** A name, a scope, and a price, with the differences legible within seconds  ✅

> **Answer:** D

### Q14. A proposal offers five options with subtle differences. The prospect says they need time to think. What should the builder expect?

- **A.** Deferral, then death: overwhelmed prospects postpone, and postponed ones fade  ✅
- **B.** A better decision: five options give diligent buyers the data for a careful pick
- **C.** An upgrade: prospects who study menus longer choose higher tiers more often
- **D.** A counteroffer: subtle differences teach prospects to draft a sixth option

### Q15. Where should granular line-item detail live, if the client genuinely needs it?

- **A.** In the Investment section, since transparency at the decision point builds trust
- **B.** In the SOW or appendix, where it informs delivery instead of inviting haggling  ✅
- **C.** In a separate email, keeping both formal documents clean of any itemization
- **D.** Nowhere: any document that itemizes at all will eventually get negotiated

- **B.** Weak proof and a vague approach: the case never justified the higher figure
- **C.** Number drift between documents, and payment terms absent from what was signed  ✅

- **B.** PDF-only sending: locked formats prevent clients from editing figures
